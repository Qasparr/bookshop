I count it a fortunate dispensation today that fate assigned to me Braunau am Inn as my birthplace. For this little town lies on the border of those two German states whose reunification appears, at least to us younger ones, as a task of life to be carried out with all means! German-Austria must return again to the great German motherland, and indeed not out of grounds of any economic considerations at all. No, no: even if this union, thought of economically, were indifferent, indeed even if it were harmful, it would nevertheless have to take place.

[kap1:1]
Like blood belongs in a common Reich.

[kap1:2]
The German people possesses so long no moral right to colonial-political activity, so long as it cannot even grasp its own sons in a common state. Only when the border of the Reich also encloses the last German, without being able any more to offer the security of his nourishment, does there arise, out of the need of its own people, the moral right to the acquisition of foreign ground and soil. The plow is then the sword, and out of the tears of war grows for posterity the daily bread. So this little border town seems to me to be the symbol of a great task. But also in yet another respect it towers admonishingly into our time today. More than a hundred years ago this inconspicuous nest, as the scene of a tragic misfortune seizing the whole German nation, had the privilege of being immortalized for ever in the annals of at least German history. In the time of the deepest humiliation of our fatherland, there fell there, for his Germany, hotly loved even in misfortune, the Nuremberger Johannes Palm, burgher bookseller, stubborn "nationalist" and Frenchman-hater.

[kap1:3]
In the Parents' Home

[kap1:4]
Stubbornly he had refused to give up his fellow — better, his principal — culprits. Just like Leo Schlageter. He was, however, also, exactly like this man, denounced by a government representative to France. An Augsburg police director acquired this sad glory, and so gave the model for new-German officialdom in the Reich of Herr Severing.

[kap1:5]
In this little Inn town, gilded by the rays of German martyrdom, Bavarian by blood, Austrian by state, there lived at the end of the eighties of the past century my parents; the father as a duty-true state official, the mother going up in the household and above all turned toward us children in ever equal loving care. Only little still adheres in my memory from this time, for already after a few years the father had to leave the little border town, become dear, again, to go down the Inn and take up a new post in Passau; thus in Germany itself.

[kap1:6]
But the lot of an Austrian customs official was in those days often called "wandering." Already a short time later the father came to Linz, and finally there also went into pension. Truly, "rest" was this not to mean for the old gentleman. As a son of a poor, small cottager, it had once already not suffered him at home. At not yet even thirteen years, the then little boy tied his little bundle and ran away from home, the Waldviertel. Despite the dissuasion of "experienced" village-dwellers, he had wandered to Vienna, to learn a trade there. That was in the fifties of the past century. A bitter resolve, to make oneself onto the road, into the uncertain, with three gulden of travel money. But when the thirteen-year-old had become seventeen years old, he had taken his journeyman's examination, yet had not won the contentment. Rather the opposite. The long time of the distress of that time, of eternal misery and lamentation, fortified the resolve to give up the trade now after all again, in order to become something "higher." If once, to the poor boy in the village, the Herr Pastor appeared as the epitome of all humanly attainable height, so now, in the metropolis powerfully expanding the circle of vision, the dignity of a state official. With all the tenacity of one become "old" through need and sorrow already in half childhood, the seventeen-year-old bored himself into his new resolve — and became an official.

[kap1:7]
The Little Ringleader

[kap1:8]
After almost twenty-three years, I believe, the goal was reached. Now there also seemed to be fulfilled the precondition for a vow that the poor boy had once vowed himself, namely, not to return into the dear paternal village before he had become something. Now the goal was reached; alone, out of the village no one could remember the erstwhile little lad any more, and to him himself the village had become strange. When he finally, as a fifty-six-year-old, went into retirement, he would yet not have been able to endure this rest a single day as a "do-nothing." He bought, in the vicinity of the Upper Austrian market town of Lambach, an estate, managed it, and so returned, in the cycle of a long, work-rich life, again to the origin of his fathers. In this time there formed in me probably the first ideals. The much romping-about in the open, the long way to school, as well as an association with extremely robust boys, one that especially sometimes filled the mother with bitter care, let me become anything rather than a room-sitter. Even if I now at that time made myself hardly any earnest thoughts about my future life-calling, yet my sympathy lay from the outset in no case in the line of my father's course of life. I believe that already at that time my oratorical talent schooled itself in the form of more or less penetrating disputations with my comrades. I had become a little ringleader, who in school learned easily and at that time also very well, but was otherwise pretty difficult to handle. As I in my free time in the canons' foundation at Lambach received singing instruction,

[kap1:9]
War Enthusiasm

[kap1:10]
I had the best opportunity to intoxicate myself often and often at the festive splendour of the extremely shiny church feasts. What was more natural, than that — just as once to the father the little Herr Village Pastor — now to me the Herr Abbot appeared as the most highly desirable ideal. At least temporarily this was the case. After, however, the Herr Father, with his contentious boy, was not able to value the oratorical talents, for comprehensible grounds, so as to draw from them some favourable conclusions for the future of his offspring, he could naturally also win no understanding for such youthful thoughts. Anxiously he indeed observed this discord of nature. In fact, the temporary longing after this calling then also lost itself very soon, in order now to make place for hopes better corresponding to my temperament. In pawing through the paternal library I had come across different books of military content, among them a people's edition of the Franco-German War 1870/71. It was two volumes of an illustrated journal from these years, which now became my favourite reading. It did not last long, and the great heroic fight had become to me the greatest inner experience. From now on I enthused more and more for everything that somehow hung together with war or yet with soldierdom. But also in another respect should this become of meaning for me. For the first time there was thrust upon me, if also in a still so unclear representation, the question, whether and what a distinction then between the Germans fighting these battles and the others might be? Why then did Austria not also fight along in this war, why not the father and not all the others also? Are we then not also the same as just all the other Germans? Do we then not all belong together? This problem began for the first time to burrow in my little brain.

[kap1:11]
With inner envy I had, upon cautious questions, to hear the answer, that not every German possesses the luck to belong to the Reich of Bismarck. This I could not grasp.

[kap1:12]
I Was To Study.

[kap1:13]
Calling — "Choice"

[kap1:14]
From my whole essence, and still more from my temperament, the father believed he could draw the conclusion that the humanistic Gymnasium would represent a contradiction to my endowment. Better seemed to him a Realschule to correspond. Especially was he still strengthened in this opinion by an apparent capability for drawing; an object which in the Austrian Gymnasien, in his conviction, was neglected. Perhaps, however, his own heavy life's work was also co-determining, which let him esteem humanistic study, as impractical in his eyes, less. Fundamentally, however, he was of the opinion of will that, as he, naturally also his son would become a state official, yes, must. His bitter youth let what was later achieved appear to him, quite naturally, the greater, as this was indeed only the exclusive result of his iron diligence and his own energy. It was the pride of the self-become who moved him also to want to bring his son into the same, if possible naturally higher, life position, the more so as he indeed, through the diligence of his own life, was able to facilitate the becoming for his child by so much.

[kap1:15]
The thought of a rejection of that which once became to him the content of a whole life appeared to him yet as ungraspable. So the resolve of the father was simple, determined and clear, in his own eyes self-evident.

[kap1:16]
Finally, it would also have come before him as entirely unendurable, to his nature become masterful in the bitter existence-fight of a whole life, in such things to leave the last decision, say, to the boy himself, in his eyes inexperienced and with that just not yet responsible.

[kap1:17]
Never State Official

[kap1:18]
It would also, as a bad and reprehensible weakness in the exercise of the paternal authority and responsibility falling to him for the later life of his child, have been impossible to fit with his other conception of duty-fulfilment. And yet it should come otherwise. For the first time in my life I was, as at that time scarcely an eleven-year-old, thrust into opposition. As hard and resolute as the father might be in the carrying-through of plans and intentions once taken into the eye, so set-fast and contrary was, however, also his boy in the rejection of a thought not, or only little, pleasing to him. I did not want to become an official. Neither coaxing nor "earnest" representations were able to change anything on this resistance. I did not want to become an official, no and again no. All attempts to want to awaken in me, through depictions from the father's own life, love or pleasure in this calling, turned into the opposite. It became to me yawningly nauseous at the thought, as an unfree man one day to be allowed to sit in a bureau; not to be able to be master of one's own time, but to have to squeeze into forms to be filled out the content of a whole life. What thoughts could this also awaken in a boy who really was everything else, but only not "good" in the common usage! The laughably easy learning in school gave me so much free time that the sun saw me more than the room did. When today my life is checked through by my political opponents in loving attentiveness into the time of my then youth, in order finally with relief to be able to establish what intolerable pranks this "Hitler" had perpetrated already in his youth, then I thank heaven that it still now gives me something from out of the memories of this blissful time. Meadow and forest were then the fencing-floor upon which the ever present "oppositions" came to the carrying-out.

[kap1:19]
But Rather Art Painter

[kap1:20]
Also the now following visit of the Realschule could do little to halt this. Truly, now, however, also another opposition had to be fought out. So long as the intention of the father to let me become a state official stood only against my principled disinclination toward the official-calling in itself, the conflict was easy to endure. I could so long also hold back somewhat with my inner views, needed indeed not always at once to contradict. It sufficed my own firm resolve, later once not to become an official, to calm me inwardly completely. This resolve, however, I possessed unchangeably. More heavily became the question when the father's plan was faced with one of my own. Already at twelve years this hit. How it now came, I today myself do not know, but one day it was clear to me that I would become a painter, art painter. My talent for drawing indeed stood firm; it was even one ground for the father to send me to the Realschule; alone never and never would this man have thought, say, to let me be educated professionally in such a direction. On the contrary. As I for the first time, after renewed rejection of the paternal favourite thought, got the question put, what then I actually myself wanted to become, and pretty unmediatedly blurted out with my meanwhile firmly seized resolve, the father was first speechless. "Painter? Art painter?" He doubted on my understanding, believed perhaps also not to have rightly heard or understood. After he was, however, enlightened about it and especially felt the earnestness of my intention, he threw himself then also with the whole decisiveness of his essence against it. His decision here was only very simple, in which some weighing of my perhaps really present capabilities could not at all come into question. "Art painter, no, as long as I live, never." As now, however, his son, with different other qualities, might well also have inherited one of a similar rigidity, so also a similar answer came back. Only naturally reversed in sense.

[kap1:21]
The Young Nationalist

[kap1:22]
On both sides it stayed standing with that. The father did not leave his "Never" and I strengthened my "Nevertheless."

[kap1:23]
Truly, this now had not very pleasing consequences. The old gentleman became embittered, and, so much as I loved him, so also I. The father forbade himself every hope that I would ever be educated to a painter. I went a step further and explained that I then would not learn at all any more. As I now naturally with such "explanations" yet pulled the shorter, insofar as the old gentleman now made ready to carry through his authority ruthlessly, I kept silent in future, but set my threat into reality. I believed that, when the father first saw the lacking progress in the Realschule, he, good or ill, would indeed have to let me go toward my dreamed luck. I do not know whether this reckoning would have hit. Sure was at first only my apparent failure in school. What pleased me, I learned, above all also everything that I, in my opinion, would later need as a painter. What seemed to me meaningless in this respect, or also otherwise did not attract me so, I sabotaged completely. My certificates of this time represented, according to subject and its estimation, always extremes. Next to "commendable" and "excellent," "sufficient" or also "not sufficient." By far the best were my performances in geography and still more in world history. The two favourite subjects, in which I shot ahead of the class. If I now, after so many years, hold the result of this time testingly before my eyes, so I see two outstanding facts as especially meaningful:

Firstly:

The German Ostmark

[kap1:30]
The old Austria was a "nationalities-state." The member of the German Reich could in the ground, at least at that time, not at all grasp which meaning this fact possesses for the everyday life of the individual in such a state. One had, after the wonderful victory-march of the heroic armies in the Franco-German War, gradually estranged oneself ever more from the Germandom of abroad, in part also not been able to value this any more, or indeed also not been able to. One confused especially, in respect of the German-Austrian, only too easily the depraved dynasty with the in-the-kernel primally-healthy people. One did not grasp that, if the German in Austria were really not still of the best blood, he could have possessed the power to imprint his stamp so very upon a 52-million-state, that indeed even in Germany the erroneous opinion could arise, Austria were a German state. A nonsense of the heaviest consequences, but a yet brilliant witness for the ten million Germans of the Ostmark. Of the eternal unrelenting fight for the German language, for German school and German essence, only quite few Germans out of the Reich had an intimation. Only today, as this sad need is forced upon many millions of our people out of the Reich itself, who under foreign rule dream of the common fatherland and, longing after it, at least seek to preserve the holy claim-right of the mother tongue, does one understand in a greater circle what it means to have to fight for one's folkdom. Now perhaps also the one or the other can measure the greatness of the Germandom out of the old Ostmark of the Reich, which, placed upon itself alone, for centuries first shielded the Reich toward the East, in order finally in wearing-down small-war to hold the German language-border, in a time in which the Reich indeed interested itself for colonies, but not for the own flesh and blood before its gates.

[kap1:31]
The Fight For Folkdom

[kap1:32]
As everywhere and always, in every fight, there were also in the language-fight of old Austria three layers:

[kap1:33]
the Fighters, the Lukewarm, the Traitors

[kap1:34]
Already in school this sieving began to enter. For it is the noteworthy of the language-fight in general that its waves perhaps most heavily just wash around the school, as the planting-site of the coming generation. Around the child is this fight led, and at the child is directed the first appeal of this strife: "German boy, forget not that you are a German," and "Maiden, bear in mind that you shall become a German mother!" Who knows the soul of youth, he will be able to understand that just she opens the ears most joyfully for such a fight-call. In a hundredfold forms she then tends to lead this fight, in her way and with her weapons. She declines to sing un-German songs, enthuses so much the more for German hero-greatness, the more one seeks to estrange her from this; gathers hellers, saved from the mouth, for the fight-treasure of the grown; she is unbelievably fine-eared against the un-German teacher, and withers-hairy at once; bears the forbidden badges of the own folkdom and is lucky to be punished or even struck for it. She is thus in small a faithful mirror-image of the great, only often in better and more upright disposition. Also I had so once the possibility, already in relatively early youth, to take part in the nationality-fight of old Austria. For Südmark and Schulverein was there gathered, through cornflowers and black-red-golden colours the disposition stressed, with "Heil" greeted, and instead of the Kaiser-song more gladly "Deutschland über alles" sung, despite warning and punishments. The boy became thereby politically schooled in a time in which the member of his so-called national-state mostly still knew of his folkdom little more than the language. That I at that time already did not belong to the Lukewarm, understands itself of its own accord.

Yes, as finally Germany began to separate itself into two power-domains, just this separation became German history. The Emperor-insignia, preserved at Vienna, of one-time Reich-glory seem as a wonderful charm to work further as a pledge of an eternal community. The elementary outcry of the German-Austrian people in the days of the collapse of the Habsburg state after union with the German motherland was indeed only the result of a feeling sleeping deep in the heart of the whole people, of longing after this return into the never-forgotten father-house. In it lies a well that never runs dry; that especially in times of forgetting, as a still admonisher, over momentary well-living away, will again and again, through the memory of the past, whisper of new future.

[kap1:41]
History Instruction

[kap1:42]
The instruction about world history in the so-called middle schools lies now truly also today still very much awry. Few teachers grasp that the goal just of the historical instruction never and never can lie in the learning-by-heart and down-rattling of historical dates and events; that it does not come down to it, whether the boy now exactly knows when this or that battle was struck, a field-marshal was born, or even a (mostly very insignificant) monarch got the crown of his ancestors set upon his head. No, true God, on that it comes down little. History to "learn" means: the forces to seek and to find that as causes lead to those effects which we then as historical events see before our eyes. The art of reading as of learning is also here:

[kap1:43]
Essential to keep, Inessential to forget.

[kap1:44]
It became perhaps determining for my whole later life, that luck once gave me, just for history, a teacher who understood it, as one of the very few, to make, for instruction and examination, this viewpoint into the governing one. In my then professor, Dr. Leopold Pötsch at the Realschule in Linz, was this demand embodied in truly ideal manner. An old gentleman, of as kind as also a determined appearance, he was able, especially through a blinding eloquence, not only to fetter us, but truly to sweep us along. Still today I recall myself with quiet emotion of the grey man, who in the fire of his portrayal sometimes let us forget the present, magicked us back into bygone times, and out of the fog-veil of the millennia formed the dry historical memory into living reality. We sat then there, often kindled to bright glow, at times even moved to tears. The luck became the greater as this teacher understood it to lighten the past out of the present, out of the past, however, to draw the consequences for the present. So he brought then also, more than any other, understanding for all the day-problems that at that time held us in breath. Our little national fanaticism became to him a means to our education, in that he, more often than once appealing to the national honour-feeling, thereby alone brought us urchins

History Favourite Subject

[kap1:47]
To me this teacher has made history into the favourite subject. Truly I became then already, indeed unintentionally by him, a young revolutionary.

[kap1:48]
Who could also, under such a teacher, study German history without becoming an enemy of the state which through its ruler-house influenced the fates of the nation in so baleful a manner? Who, finally, could still preserve Kaiser-loyalty against a dynasty which in past and present betrayed the concerns of the German people again and again for the sake of base own advantages? Did we not as boys already know that this Austrian state possessed no love for us Germans, indeed in general could not possess any at all? The historical cognition of the workings of the Habsburg house was still supported by the daily experience. In the North and in the South the foreign peoples-poison ate on the body of our folkdom, and even Vienna became visibly more and more an un-German city. The enormous burdens that one imposed on the German people, the unheard-of sacrifices of it in taxes and in blood — and yet every not entirely blind man had to recognise that this all would be for nothing. What at that most pained us was, moreover, the fact that this whole system was morally covered through the alliance with Germany, with which the slow extermination of the Germandom in the old monarchy also still, in a certain measure, was given the sanction from Germany itself.

Historical Cognitions

[kap1:50]

Only in the Reich itself the at that time already solely "called" saw of all this nothing. As struck with blindness they walked at the side of a corpse, and believed in the indications of the decay still to discover signs of "new" life. World history became to me more and more an inexhaustible spring of the understanding for the historical acting of the present, thus for politics. I will thereby not "learn" it; rather, it shall teach me.

Wagner-Veneration

[kap1:55]
Was I so early become a political "revolutionary," so not less early also an artistic one. The Austrian state capital possessed at that time a relatively not bad theatre. There was played pretty much everything. At twelve years I saw there for the first time "Wilhelm Tell"; a few months later, as the first opera of my life, "Lohengrin." With one stroke I was fettered. The youthful enthusiasm for the Bayreuth master knew no boundaries. Thereon neither begging nor threatening could change anything more. I wanted to become a painter and, around no power of the world, an official. It was only strange that with rising years there set in ever more interest for architecture. I held this at that time for the self-evident completion of my painterly capability and rejoiced only inwardly over the expansion of my artistic frame. That it should one day come otherwise, I did not suspect.

A stroke hit the otherwise still so able-bodied gentleman and ended, in the most painless manner, his earthly wandering, sinking us all into deepest sorrow.

Death of the Parents

[kap1:59]
What he longed for most, to co-create his child's existence, so as to preserve it thereby before his own bitter becoming, seemed to him at that time probably not to have succeeded. Alone he laid, if also entirely unconsciously, the seeds for a future which at that time neither he nor I would have grasped. At first there changed outwardly, indeed, nothing. The mother felt herself probably obliged, according to the wish of the father, to lead my education further, that is, to let me study for the official-career. It was the happiest days, which appeared to me almost as a beautiful dream; and a dream it should indeed only be. Two years later the death of the mother made to all the beautiful plans a sudden end. It was the conclusion of a long, painful sickness which from the beginning let little hope of healing arise. Nonetheless the blow hit me especially tremendously. I had revered the father, the mother, however, loved. Need and hard actuality forced me now to seize a quick resolve.

Removal to Vienna

[kap1:61]
The slight paternal means had been used up to a large part through the heavy sickness of the mother; the orphan-pension falling to me sufficed not, in order even only to be able to live; so I was now referred to earning myself somehow my bread myself. A suitcase with clothes and linen in the hands, with an unshakeable will in the heart, I drove so to Vienna. What had succeeded to the father 50 years before, I also hoped to wrest from fate; also I wanted to become "something" — however, under no circumstance an official.
