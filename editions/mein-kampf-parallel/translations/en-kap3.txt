[kap3:0]
I am today of the conviction that the man, in general, cases of quite special giftedness excepted, should not be publicly active in politics before his thirtieth year. He should not do this, since indeed up to into this time there mostly first takes place the formation of a general platform, from out of which he now examines the various political problems and finally lays down his own position toward them. Only after the winning of such a fundamental world-view and the steadiness of one's own manner of consideration, attained thereby, toward the individual questions of the day should or may the man, now at least inwardly matured, take part in the political leadership of the commonwealth.

[kap3:1]
If this is otherwise, then he runs the danger of one day either having to change his hitherto position in essential questions, or, against his better knowledge and insight, to remain standing at an outlook which understanding and conviction have already long since rejected. In the first case this is very embarrassing for him personally, since he now, as himself wavering, may no longer with right expect that the belief of his followers belong to him in like unshakeable firmness as before; for those led by him, however, such a falling-away of the leader means perplexity, and not seldom the feeling of a certain shamedness toward those hitherto fought by them. In the second case, however, there enters what we especially today see so often: in exactly the measure in which the leader no longer believes in what was said by him, his defence becomes hollow and flat, but base in the choice of the means. While he himself no longer thinks of seriously standing up for his political revelations (one does not die for something in which one oneself does not believe), the demands upon his followers, however, become in exactly this proportion ever greater and more shameless, until he finally sacrifices the last rest of the leader in order to land at the "politician"; that is, at that sort of men whose only real disposition is dispositionlessness, paired with impudent intrusiveness and an often shamelessly developed art of lying.

[kap3:2]
The Politician

[kap3:3]
If such a fellow then, to the misfortune of decent humanity, also comes into a parliament, then one should know already from the beginning that the essence of politics for him consists now only in the heroic fight for the lasting possession of this milk-bottle of his life and of his family. The more then wife and child hang upon it, the more tenaciously will he fight for his mandate. Every other man with political instincts is by that alone already his personal enemy; in every new movement he scents the possible beginning of his end, and in every greater man the danger probably one day threatening from him.

[kap3:4]
I shall yet come to speak thoroughly of this sort of parliament-bedbugs.

[kap3:5]
The thirty-year-old, too, will in the course of his life still have much to learn; alone it will be only a completion and filling-out of the frame which the world-view, fundamentally adopted, presents to him. His learning will no longer be a principled re-learning, but a learning-in-addition, and his followers will not have to swallow down the oppressed feeling of having hitherto been falsely instructed by him, but on the contrary: the visible organic growing of the leader will grant them satisfaction, since his learning indeed means only the deepening of their own doctrine. This, however, is in their eyes a proof of the correctness of their hitherto outlooks.

[kap3:6]
Political Thinking

[kap3:7]
A leader who must leave the platform of his general world-view in itself, because recognized as false, acts only then with decency when he is ready, in the knowledge of his hitherto faulty insight, to draw the last consequence. He must in such a case renounce at least the public exercise of a further political activity. For since he already once fell to an error in fundamental insights, the possibility is also given a second time. On no account, however, has he still the right to take up further the trust of his fellow-citizens or even to demand such.

[kap3:8]
How little, however, such a decency is today complied with, is attested only by the general depravity of the pack that at this time feels itself called to "make" politics.

[kap3:9]
Scarcely one of them is chosen for it.

[kap3:10]
I had once guarded myself against stepping forward publicly in any way, although I believe myself to have busied myself more with politics than so many others. Only in the smallest circle did I speak of what inwardly moved or attracted me. This speaking in the narrowest frame had much good for itself: I learned so indeed less "to speak," but for that the men in their often infinitely primitive outlooks and objections to know. Thereby I schooled myself, without losing time and possibility, toward my own further formation. The opportunity for this was surely nowhere in Germany so favorable as then in Vienna.

[kap3:11]
The general political thinking in the old Danube monarchy was first, in its extent, greater and more encompassing than in the old Germany of the same time — parts of Prussia, Hamburg, and the coast of the North Sea excepted. I understand, however, under the designation "Austria" in this case that territory of the great Habsburg realm which, in consequence of its German settlement, in every respect was not only the historical occasion of the formation of this state at all, but which in its population also exclusively displayed that force,

[kap3:12]
Vienna's Last Upswing

[kap3:13]
which to this politically so artificial structure was able to present the inner cultural life for many centuries. The more the time progressed, the more were the standing and future of this state dependent precisely upon the preservation of this germ-cell of the realm.

[kap3:14]
If the old hereditary lands were the heart of the realm, which ever again drove fresh blood into the circulation of state and cultural life, then, however, was Vienna brain and will at once.

[kap3:15]
Already in its outer arrangement one might ascribe to this city the force to throne, in such a conglomerate of peoples, as a uniting queen, so as thereby through the splendour of its own beauty to let the evil appearances of age of the whole be forgotten.

[kap3:16]
Might the realm in its interior still so fiercely twitch under the bloody fights of the individual nationalities, the foreign country, and especially Germany, saw only the lovable picture of this city. The deception was so much the greater, as Vienna in this time seemed perhaps to take the last and greatest visible upswing. Under the rule of a truly genial mayor the venerable residence of the emperors of the old realm awoke once more to a wonderful young life. The last great German whom the colonist-people of the Ostmark bore out of its ranks counted officially not among the so-called "statesmen"; but in that this Dr. Lueger, as mayor of the "Reich capital and residence city" Vienna, conjured forth one unheard-of achievement after another upon, one may say, all domains of communal economic and cultural politics, he strengthened the heart of the entire realm and became over this detour a greater statesman than the so-called "diplomats" all together then were. If the structure of peoples called "Austria" nevertheless finally perished, then this speaks not in the least against the political capability of the Germandom in the old Ostmark, but was the compelling result of the impossibility of being able to hold, with ten million men, a fifty-million state of different nations in the length, if precisely quite definite presuppositions were not given in time.

[kap3:17]
The Germandom in Austria

[kap3:18]
The German-Austrian thought more than big.

[kap3:19]
He was always accustomed to live in the frame of a great realm, and had never lost the feeling for the tasks bound up with it. He was the only one in this state who, beyond the borders of the narrower crown-land, still saw the border of the realm; indeed, when fate should finally separate him from the common fatherland, then he still always tried to become master of the monstrous task and to preserve for the Germandom what the fathers once in endless fights had wrested from the East. Whereby it is still to be considered that this could happen only with divided force; for the heart and memory of the best never ceased to feel for the common motherland, and only a rest remained to the homeland.

[kap3:20]
Already the general circle of vision of the German-Austrian was a relatively wide one. His economic relations often comprised nearly the whole many-shaped realm. Almost all really great undertakings found themselves in his hands; the leading personnel of technicians and officials was for the greatest part furnished by him. But he was also the bearer of foreign trade, so far as Jewry had not laid its hand upon this most-original domain of its own. Politically he alone still held the state together. Already the service time with the army led him far out beyond the narrow borders of the homeland. The German-Austrian recruit indeed perhaps marched in with a German regiment, alone the regiment itself could lie just as well in the Herzegovina as in Vienna or Galicia. The officer corps was always still German, the higher officialdom predominantly. German, however, were finally art and science. Apart from the kitsch of the newer development of art, whose production might indeed also be possible to a Negro people without further ado, the owner and also spreader of true artistic disposition was only the German alone.

[kap3:21]
In music, architecture, sculpture, and painting, Vienna was the fountain which, in inexhaustible fullness, supplied the whole Double Monarchy, without ever itself visibly drying up.

[kap3:22]
The Germandom was finally still the bearer of the entire foreign policy, if one disregards the Hungarians, few in number.

[kap3:23]
Nevertheless was every attempt to preserve this realm in vain, since the most essential presupposition was lacking.

[kap3:24]
For the Austrian state of peoples there was only one possibility of overcoming the centrifugal forces among the individual nations. The state was either centrally governed and therewith but also just as much inwardly organized, or it was not thinkable at all.

[kap3:25]
In various light moments this insight also came to the "Most High" place, but to be mostly already after a short time forgotten again or laid aside as hard to carry through. Every thought of a more federative shaping of the realm had compellingly to fail in consequence of the lack of a strong state germ-cell of overwhelming might. To this there came still the essentially other inner presuppositions of the Austrian state as against the German Reich of Bismarckian shaping. In Germany it was only a matter of overcoming political traditions, since culturally a common foundation was always present. Above all, the Reich possessed, apart from small foreign splinters, only members of one people.

[kap3:26]
In Austria the relations lay reversed.

[kap3:27]
Here the political memory of own greatness among the individual lands, Hungary excepted, either fell away entirely, or it was deleted by the sponge of time, at least, however, blurred and indistinct. For that there now developed, in the age of the nationality principle, in the various lands folkish forces whose overcoming had to become difficult in exactly the measure as at the edge of the monarchy national states began to form, whose state-peoples, racially related or equal to the individual Austrian people-splinters, now on their part were able to exercise more power of attraction than this, conversely, was still possible to the German-Austrian.

[kap3:28]
Even Vienna could in the length no longer withstand this fight.

[kap3:29]
Centrifugal Forces of the Peoples of Austria

[kap3:30]
With the development of Budapest into a great city it had for the first time received a rivaless whose task was no longer the holding-together of the whole monarchy, but rather the strengthening of one part of the same.

[kap3:31]
In a short time already Prague was to follow the example, then Lemberg, Laibach, etc. With the ascent of these sometime provincial cities to national capitals of individual lands there now also formed themselves centres for a more and more independent cultural life of the same. Only thereby, however, did the folkish-political instincts receive their spiritual foundation and deepening. So there once had to approach the point of time at which these driving forces of the individual peoples became mightier than the force of the common interests, and then it was done for with Austria.

[kap3:32]
This development could, since the death of Joseph II, be established very clearly in its course. Its speed was dependent on a series of factors which partly lay in the monarchy itself, but partly, however, formed the result of the respective foreign-political position of the realm. If one wanted to take up and fight through the fight for the preservation of this state in earnest, then only an equally ruthless as persistent centralization alone could lead to the goal. Then, however, there had above all, through the principled laying-down of a unitary state language, to be stressed the purely formal belonging-together, but to the administration the technical means had to be pressed into the hand, without which a unitary state simply cannot exist. Likewise only then could, in the length, through school and instruction a unitary state disposition be bred up. This was not to be reached in ten or twenty years, but here one had to reckon with centuries, as indeed in general in all colonisatorial questions persistence carries a greater significance than the energy of the moment.

[kap3:33]
That then the administration as well as the political leadership are to be led in strictest unitariness is self-evident.

[kap3:34]
Consequences of Blood-Difference

[kap3:35]
It was now for me infinitely instructive to establish why this did not happen, or better, why one did not do this. Only the one guilty of this omission was the one guilty of the collapse of the realm.

[kap3:36]
The old Austria was more than another state bound to the greatness of its leadership. Here there lacked, indeed, the foundation of the national state, which in the folkish foundation still always possesses a force of preservation, if the leadership as such ever so much fails. The unitary people-state can, by virtue of the natural inertia of its inhabitants and the resistance force bound up therewith, sometimes endure astonishingly long periods of worst administration or leadership, without perishing inwardly thereby. It is then often so, as if in such a body there were no life at all any more, as if it were dead and departed, until suddenly the one-believed-dead raises himself again and now gives astonishing signs of his indestructible life-force to the rest of humanity.

[kap3:37]
But otherwise is this with a realm which, composed out of not equal peoples, is held not by the common blood, but rather by a common fist. Here every weakness of the leadership will not lead to a winter-sleep of the state, but will give occasion to an awakening of all the individual instincts which are present in blood, without being able to unfold themselves in times of an overwhelming will. Only through centuries-long common education, through common tradition, common interests, etc., can this danger be mitigated. Therefore such state structures, the younger they are, will so much the more depend on the greatness of the leadership; indeed, as the work of overwhelming force-men and spirit-heroes, will often already after the death of the lonely great founder again fall apart. But even after centuries these dangers cannot count as overcome; they only slumber, to awake often quite suddenly, as soon as the weakness of the common leadership and the force of education, the sublimity of all tradition, no longer is able to overcome the swing of the own life-urge of the various tribes.

[kap3:38]
This not to have grasped is perhaps the tragic guilt of the House of Habsburg.

[kap3:39]
Joseph II

[kap3:40]
To a single one among them fate held once more the torch up over the future of his land; then it went out for ever.

[kap3:41]
Joseph II, Roman emperor of the German nation, saw in flying fear how his house, pressed to the uttermost edge of the realm, would one day have to vanish in the whirlpool of a Babylon of peoples, if the neglected of the fathers was not made good again in the last hour. With superhuman force the "Friend of Men" stemmed himself against the carelessness of the ancestors and sought in one decade to catch up what centuries before had neglected. If only forty years had been granted to him for his work, and if after him also only two generations had carried forward the begun work in like manner, then the miracle would probably have succeeded. But when he, after scarcely ten years of government, worn down in body and soul, died, there sank with him also his work into the grave, in order, never again reawakened, to fall asleep for ever in the Capuchin crypt.

[kap3:42]
His successors were equal to the task neither spiritually nor in will.

[kap3:43]
The Dissolution of the Danube Monarchy

[kap3:44]
When now through Europe the first revolutionary weather-signs of a new time flamed, then Austria, too, began slowly, little by little, to catch fire. Alone when the fire finally broke out, then the glow was already fanned less by social, societal, or even general political causes than rather by driving forces of folkish origin.

[kap3:45]
The revolution of the year 1848 could everywhere be class fight; in Austria, however, it was already the beginning of a new race-strife. In that the German then, forgetting or not recognizing this origin, placed himself into the service of the revolutionary uprising, he therewith sealed his own lot. He helped to awaken the spirit of western democracy, which in a short time withdrew from him the foundations of his own existence.

[kap3:46]
With the formation of a parliamentary body of representation without the preceding laying-down and consolidation of a common state language, the foundation stone to the end of the predominance of the Germandom in the monarchy had been laid. From this moment on, however, the state itself was therewith also lost. All that now still followed was only the historical unwinding of a realm.

[kap3:47]
To pursue this dissolution was as shattering as instructive. In a thousand and again a thousand forms there fulfilled itself in the individual this execution of a historical judgment. That a great part of men wandered blindly through the appearances of decay proved only the will of the gods to Austria's annihilation.

[kap3:48]
I will not lose myself here in details, since this is not the task of this book. I will only draw into the circle of a more thorough consideration those processes which, as ever-equal causes of the decay of peoples and states, possess significance also for our time today, and which finally helped to secure the foundations of my political manner of thinking.

[kap3:49]
Parliamentarism

[kap3:50]
Among the institutions which could most clearly point out the gnawing-away of the Austrian monarchy even to the philistine otherwise not blessed with sharp eyes, there stood at the head that which should call the most strength its own — the Parliament or, as it was called in Austria, the Reichsrat.

[kap3:51]
Evidently the model of this body lay in England, the land of classical "democracy." From there one took over the whole blissful arrangement and set it as unchanged as possible to Vienna. In the House of Deputies and the House of Lords the English two-chamber system celebrated its resurrection. Only the "houses" themselves were somewhat different. When Barry once let his parliament palace grow up out of the floods of the Thames, he reached into the history of the British world empire and fetched out of it the adornment for the 1200 niches, consoles, and columns of his splendour-building. In sculptural work and the painter's art the house thus became to the Lords and to the people the temple of fame of the nation.

[kap3:52]
Here came the first difficulty for Vienna. For when the Dane Hansen had completed the last gables on the marble house of the new representation of the people, there remained to him, for adornment, nothing else over than to attempt borrowings from antiquity. Roman and Greek statesmen and philosophers now beautify this theatre-building of "western democracy," and in symbolic irony the quadrigas over the two houses draw apart toward the four directions of heaven, in such a manner lending to the doings of that time in the interior also outwardly the best expression.

[kap3:53]
The "nationalities" had forbidden it to themselves as insult and provocation that in this work Austrian history would be glorified, just as in the Reich itself, indeed, one only under the thunder of the World War battles dared to consecrate Wallot's building of the Reichstag through inscription to the German people.

[kap3:54]
When I, not yet twenty years old, went for the first time into the splendour-building on the Franzensring, in order to attend as spectator and hearer a sitting of the House of Deputies, I was seized by the most conflicting feelings.

[kap3:55]
I had already from ever hated the Parliament, yet by no means as institution in itself. On the contrary, as a freedom-feeling man I could

[kap3:56]
imagine to myself no other possibility of government at all, for the thought of any dictatorship would, with my attitude toward the House of Habsburg, have appeared to me as a crime against freedom and against every reason.

[kap3:57]
Not little did there contribute to it that to me as a young man, in consequence of my much newspaper reading, without my perhaps myself suspecting this, a certain admiration for the English Parliament had been inoculated, which I was not able to lose so without further ado. The dignity with which there also the Lower House lay over its tasks (as our press understood so beautifully to portray this) imposed mightily upon me. Could there then at all be a more sublime form of the self-government of a people-body?

[kap3:58]
Precisely therefore, however, was I an enemy of the Austrian Parliament. I held the form of the whole appearance to be unworthy of the great model. Now, however, the following still came to it:

[kap3:59]
The fate of the Germandom in the Austrian state was dependent on its position in the Reichsrat. Up to the introduction of the universal and secret right of vote there was still present a, if also insignificant, German majority in the Parliament. Already this condition was questionable, since with the nationally unreliable attitude of the Social Democracy, this, in critical questions concerning the Germandom — in order not to make the followers in the individual foreign peoples estranged from itself — always stepped up against the German concerns. The Social Democracy already then could not be regarded as a German party. With the introduction of the universal right of vote, however, the German superiority ceased also purely in numbers. Now there lay no more hindrance in the way of the further de-Germanization of the state.

[kap3:60]
The national instinct of self-preservation let me already then, out of this ground, little love a representation of the people in which the Germandom always, instead of represented, was betrayed. Alone these were defects which, like so much else indeed also, were to be ascribed not to the thing in itself, but to the Austrian state. I believed earlier still that with a restoration of the German majority in the bodies of representation, no occasion would any more be present for a principled position against it, so long as the old state indeed still existed at all.

[kap3:61]
So, then, inwardly adjusted, I entered for the first time the as much hallowed as contested rooms. To be sure, they were hallowed to me only through the

[kap3:62]
sublime beauty of the magnificent building. A Hellenic wonder-work on German soil.

[kap3:63]
In how short a time, however, was I outraged, when I saw the miserable spectacle which now unrolled itself under my eyes!

[kap3:64]
There were some hundred of these representatives of the people present, who just had to take a position toward a question of important economic significance.

[kap3:65]
Already this first day sufficed me, in order to stimulate me to thinking for weeks through.

[kap3:66]
The spiritual content of what was brought forward lay on a truly depressing "height," so far as one could understand the talk at all; for some of the gentlemen spoke not German, but in their Slavic mother-tongues or better dialects. What I until then knew only from the reading of the newspapers, I now had opportunity to hear with my own ears. A gesticulating, in all pitches screaming-through-one-another, wildly-moved mass; above it a harmless old uncle who, in the sweat of his countenance, endeavoured, through hefty swinging of a bell and now soothing, now admonishing earnest calls, to bring the dignity of the house again into flow.

[kap3:67]
I had to laugh.

[kap3:68]
Some weeks later I was anew in the house. The picture was changed, not to be recognized again. The hall quite empty. One slept down there. Some deputies were on their places and yawned at one another; one "spoke." A vice-president of the house was present and looked evidently bored into the hall.

[kap3:69]
The first doubts rose up in me. Now I ran, if the time only somehow offered me the possibility, ever again there and considered to myself, still and attentive, the respective picture, heard the speeches, so far as they were to be understood, studied the more or less intelligent faces of these Chosen of the nations of this sad state — and then gradually made to myself my own thoughts.

[kap3:70]
One year of this quiet observation sufficed to change or to remove my earlier view of the essence of this institution also completely. My interior no longer took a position against the misshapen form which this thought had assumed in Austria; no, now I could no longer recognize the Parliament as such. Until then I saw the misfortune of the Austrian Parliament in the lack of a German majority; now, however,

[kap3:71]
I saw the doom in the whole kind and essence of this institution at large.

[kap3:72]
A whole series of questions rose up in me then.

[kap3:73]
I began to make myself familiar with the democratic principle of majority-determination, as the foundation of this whole institution, but also bestowed no less attention upon the spiritual and moral values of the gentlemen who, as the Chosen of the nations, should serve this purpose.

[kap3:74]
So I learned to know institutions and bearers of the same at once.

[kap3:75]
In the course of some years there then formed itself to me, in knowledge and insight, the type of the most dignified appearance of the newer time in plastic clarity: the parliamentarian. He began to imprint himself on me in a form which was never more subjected to an essential change.

[kap3:76]
Also this time the instruction by spectacle of practical reality had preserved me from suffocating in a theory which on the first glance appears seductive to so many, which, however, nevertheless is to be reckoned among the decay-appearances of humanity.

[kap3:77]
The democracy of today's West is the forerunner of Marxism, which without it would not be thinkable at all. It gives first to this world-pest the nutrient soil on which the plague can then spread itself. In its outward form of expression, parliamentarism, it created for itself still a "mock-birth out of dirt and fire," at which to me only unfortunately the "fire" seems at the moment to have burned out.

[kap3:78]
I must be more than thankful to fate that it presented to me also this question still in Vienna for examination, for I fear that I in Germany then would have made the answer too easy for myself. Had I learned to know the ridiculousness of this institution, called "Parliament," first in Berlin, then I would perhaps have fallen into the opposite and placed myself, not without apparently good ground, on the side of those who beheld the people's and the Reich's salvation in the exclusive furthering of the might of the emperor-thought alone, and so nevertheless stood toward the time and toward men foreign and blind at once.

[kap3:79]
In Austria this was impossible.

[kap3:80]
Here one could not so easily fall from one error into the other. If the Parliament was good for nothing, then the Habsburgs were good for still much less — on no account more. With the rejection of "parliamentarism" alone it was not done here; for then there always still remained the question open:

[kap3:81]
What now? The rejection and removal of the Reichsrat would as sole governing power have left over only the House of Habsburg, a thought for me especially quite unbearable.

[kap3:82]
The difficulty of this special case led me to a more thorough consideration of the problem in itself, than this otherwise probably would have entered in so young years.

[kap3:83]
The Lack of Responsibility

[kap3:84]
What gave me first of all and most of all to think was the evident lack of every responsibility of a single person.

[kap3:85]
The Parliament makes any decision, whose consequences may be ever so devastating — nobody bears for it a responsibility, nobody can ever be drawn to account. For does this perhaps mean to take over responsibility, when after a collapse without equal the guilty government steps back? Or the coalition changes itself, indeed the Parliament dissolves itself?

[kap3:86]
Can then a wavering majority of men ever at all be made responsible?

[kap3:87]
Is then not the thought of every responsibility bound to the person? But can one practically make the leading person of a government liable for actions whose becoming and execution are to be set exclusively upon the account of the willing and the inclination of a manifoldness of men?

[kap3:88]
Or: Is not the task of the leading statesman seen, instead of in the birth of the creative thought or plan in itself, rather only in the art of making the genius of his drafts understandable to a flock of sheep of hollow-heads, in order then to beg for their gracious consent?

[kap3:89]
Is this the criterion of the statesman, that he possesses the art of persuasion in just as high a measure as that of statesmanly cleverness in the making of great guide-lines or decisions?

[kap3:90]
Is the incapability of a leader proved thereby that it does not succeed to him to win the majority of a heap, banged together by more or less clean accidents, for a definite idea?

[kap3:91]
Yes, has this heap then ever at all grasped an idea before the success became the proclaimer of its greatness?

[kap3:92]
Is not every genial deed on this world the visible protest of genius against the inertia of the mass?

[kap3:93]
But what shall the statesman do, to whom it does not succeed to coax the favour of this heap for his plans?

[kap3:94]
Shall he buy it?

[kap3:95]
The Destruction of the Leader-Thought

[kap3:96]
Or shall he, in view of the stupidity of his fellow-citizens, renounce the carrying-out of the tasks recognized as life-necessities, withdraw himself, or shall he nevertheless remain?

[kap3:97]
Does not in such a case the real character come into an unsolvable conflict between insight and decency or, better said, honest disposition?

[kap3:98]
Where lodges here the border which divides the duty toward the generality from the obligation of personal honour?

[kap3:99]
Must not every truthful leader embitter it to himself to be degraded in such a manner to a political shover?

[kap3:100]
And must not conversely every shover now feel himself called to "make" politics, since the last responsibility is never to be borne by him, but by some ungraspable heap?

[kap3:101]
Must not our parliamentary majority-principle lead to the demolition of the leader-thought at large?

[kap3:102]
But does one believe that the progress of this world stems somehow from the brain of majorities and not from the heads of individuals?

[kap3:103]
Or does one perhaps intend, for the future, to be able to dispense with this presupposition of human culture?

[kap3:104]
Does it not seem on the contrary today to be more necessary than ever?

[kap3:105]
In that the parliamentary principle of majority-determination rejects the authority of the person and sets in its place the number of the respective heap, it sins against the aristocratic fundamental thought of nature, whereby, however, her view of nobility needs in no manner to be embodied in the present decadence of our upper ten thousand.

[kap3:106]
The Switching-Off of Heads

[kap3:107]
Which devastations this institution of modern democratic parliament-rule causes, the reader of Jewish newspapers can, to be sure, hardly imagine, so far as he has not learned to think and examine independently.

[kap3:108]
It is in the first line the occasion for the unbelievable flooding of the whole political life with the most inferior appearances of our days. As much as the truthful leader will withdraw from a political activity which in its greatest part can consist not in creative achievement and work, but rather in the haggling and dealing for the favour of a majority, so much will precisely this activity correspond to the small spirit and hence also attract this.

[kap3:109]
The more dwarfish such a quill-trader today is in spirit and capability, the clearer his own insight brings the miserableness of his actual appearance to his consciousness, the more will he praise a system which from him does not at all demand the force and geniality of a giant, but rather is content with the slyness of a village mayor; indeed, prefers to see such a kind of wisdom rather than that of a Pericles. Thereby such a drip need never torment himself with the responsibility of his working. In that the Habsburgs tried with all means to get at the body of the Germandom, this party attacked the "sublime" ruling house itself, and indeed ruthlessly. It is its merit to have redeemed the magnificent concept of fatherland-love out of the embrace of this sad dynasty.

[kap3:204]
Its following in the first time of its appearance was extraordinarily great, indeed threatened to become a formal avalanche. Alone, the success did not hold on. When I came to Vienna, the movement was already long since outstripped by the Christian-Social Party, in the meantime come to power, indeed pressed down to a nearly complete meaninglessness.

[kap3:205]
This whole process of the becoming and passing of the pan-German movement on the one side and of the unheard-of ascent of the Christian-Social Party on the other side was to become, as a classical object of study for me, of deepest significance.

[kap3:206]
When I came to Vienna, my sympathies stood full and wholly on the side of the pan-German direction.

[kap3:207]
That one brought up the courage to utter in the Parliament the call "Hoch Hohenzollern" imposed upon me just as much as it gladdened me; that one still always regarded oneself as a merely temporarily separated component part of the German Reich and let no moment pass in order to profess this also publicly, awoke in me joyful confidence; that one in all questions concerning the Germandom ruthlessly confessed colour and never let oneself be brought to compromises seemed to me the only still passable way to the rescue of our people; that, however, the movement after its first so magnificent ascent now sank down so much, I could not understand. Still less, however, that the Christian-Social Party in this same time was able to arrive at so monstrous power. It was then precisely arrived at the summit of its fame.

[kap3:208]
Schönerer and Lueger

[kap3:209]
In that I proceeded to compare both movements, fate gave me also here, accelerated through my otherwise sad situation, the best instruction for the understanding of the causes of this riddle.

[kap3:210]
I begin my weighing first with the two men who are to be regarded as leader and founder of the two parties: Georg v. Schönerer and Dr. Karl Lueger.

[kap3:211]
Regarded purely humanly, they tower, the one as the other, far beyond the frame and the extent of the so-called parliamentary appearances. In the swamp of a general political corruption their whole life remained pure and unassailable. Nevertheless my personal sympathy stood first on the side of the pan-German Schönerer, in order to turn itself only little by little also toward the Christian-Social leader.

[kap3:212]
In their capabilities compared, Schönerer seemed to me already then as the better and more thorough thinker in principled problems. He has recognized the compelling end of the Austrian state more rightly and clearly than any other. Would one especially in the Reich have heard his warnings before the Habsburg monarchy better, then the misfortune of the World War of Germany against whole Europe would never have come.

[kap3:213]
Alone if Schönerer recognized the problems according to their inner essence, then he erred so much the more in men.

[kap3:214]
Here lay again the strength of Dr. Lueger.

[kap3:215]
This one was a rare knower of men, who especially guarded himself against seeing men better than they now once are. Therefore he also reckoned more with the real possibilities of life, while Schönerer for this brought up only little understanding. All that the pan-German also thought was, taken theoretically, right; alone in that the force and the understanding lacked to convey the theoretical knowledge to the mass, therefore to bring it into such a form that it corresponded thereby to the receptivity of the broad people, which now once is and remains a limited one, all knowledge was just seer-wisdom, without ever being able to become practical reality.

[kap3:216]
This lack of actual knowledge of men led, however, in the further course to an error in the estimation of force of whole movements as well as of age-old institutions.

[kap3:217]
Finally Schönerer had, to be sure, recognized that it is here a matter of world-view questions, but not grasped that as bearer of such nearly religious convictions in the first line always only the broad masses of a people are fit.

[kap3:218]
He saw in unfortunately only very small extent the extraordinary limitedness of the fighting-will of the so-called "bourgeois" circles already in consequence of their economic position, which lets the individual fear to lose too much and therefore also holds him more back.

[kap3:219]
And yet in general a world-view will have prospect of victory only when the broad mass declares itself ready, as beareress of the new doctrine, to take the necessary fight upon itself.

[kap3:220]
To this lack of understanding for the significance of the lower people-strata there sprang then, however, also the completely insufficient conception of the social question.

[kap3:221]
In all this Dr. Lueger was the opposite of Schönerer.

[kap3:222]
The thorough knowledge of men let him judge the possible forces just as rightly as he thereby, however, also remained preserved from a too low estimation of present institutions; indeed perhaps precisely out of this ground he learned rather still to make use of such as auxiliary means for the attainment of his intentions.

[kap3:223]
He understood also only too exactly that the political fighting force of the upper bourgeoisie in today's time was only low and not sufficient to fight out the victory of a new great movement. Therefore he laid the chief weight of his political activity upon the winning of strata whose existence was threatened and which hence rather became a spur than a paralysis of the fighting-will. Just as much was he inclined to make use of all the once already present means of might, to make powerful existing institutions inclined to himself, in order to be able to draw from such old force-sources the greatest possible use for his own movement.

[kap3:224]
So he placed his new party in the first line upon the middle estate threatened by downfall and secured for himself thereby a following only very hard to shake, of just as great readiness for sacrifice as tenacious fighting force. But his infinitely cleverly shaped relation to the Catholic Church won for him in a short time the younger clergy in an extent that the old clerical party either had to clear the battlefield

[kap3:225]
was forced, or, still cleverer, joined itself to the new party, in order so to win again slowly position by position.

[kap3:226]
If, however, this alone were regarded as the characteristic essence of the man, then heavy injustice would be done to him. For to the clever tactician there came also the properties of a truly great and genial reformer. To be sure, also here limited by an exact knowledge of the possibilities now once present as well as of the capability of his own person.

[kap3:227]
It was an infinitely practical goal that this truly significant man had set himself. He wanted to conquer Vienna. Vienna was the heart of the monarchy; from this city there went still forth the last life into the sickly and aged-grown body of the rotten Reich. The healthier the heart became, so much the fresher must also the rest of the body revive. A principally right thought, which, however, could come to application only for a definite, limited time.

[kap3:228]
Causes of Schönerer's Ill-Success

[kap3:229]
And herein lay the weakness of this man.

[kap3:230]
What he achieved as mayor of the city of Vienna is in the best sense of the word immortal; the monarchy, however, he was thereby no longer able to save — it was too late.

[kap3:231]
This his adversary Schönerer had seen more clearly.

[kap3:232]
What Dr. Lueger practically attacked succeeded in wonderful manner; what he hoped for himself from it stayed out.

[kap3:233]
What Schönerer wanted did not succeed to him; what he feared, however, unfortunately struck in in terrible manner.

[kap3:234]
So both men have not reached their further goal. Lueger could no longer save Austria, and Schönerer no longer preserve the German people from decline.

[kap3:235]
It is infinitely instructive for our time today to study the causes of the failure of both parties. This is especially expedient for my friends, since in many points the relations today are similar to those of then, and errors can thereby be avoided which already once had led to the end of the one movement and to the fruitlessness of the other. The collapse of the pan-German movement in Austria had in my eyes three causes:

[kap3:236]
First, the unclear conception of the significance of the social problem precisely for a new party, revolutionary according to its inner essence.

[kap3:237]
In that Schönerer and his following turned themselves in the first line to the bourgeois strata, the result could only be a very feeble, tame one.

[kap3:238]
The German bourgeoisie is especially in its higher circles, if also unsuspected by individuals, pacifist up to formal self-denial, when it is a matter of inner affairs of the nation or of the state.

[kap3:239]
In good times, that is in this case therefore in times of a good government, such a disposition is a ground of the extraordinary worth of these strata for the state; in times of worse rule, however, it works downright devastatingly. Already in order to make the carrying-through of a really earnest fight possible at all, the pan-German movement had above all to devote itself to the winning of the masses. That it did not do this took from it from

[kap3:240]
the outset the elementary swing which such a wave now once needs, if it is not already in a short time to ebb away.

[kap3:241]
As soon as, however, this principle is not from the beginning taken into the eye and also carried through, the new party loses for later every possibility of a catching-up of the neglected. For with the taking-up of exceedingly numerous moderate-bourgeois elements, the inner adjustment of the movement will always direct itself according to these and so lose every further prospect for the winning of noteworthy forces out of the broad people. Therewith, however, such a movement will no more come out beyond mere nagging and criticizing. The more or less almost religious belief, bound up with a just such readiness for sacrifice, will nevermore be to be found; in its place, however, there will step the striving, through "positive" co-work, that is in this case however through recognition of the given, to grind off gradually the hardnesses of the fight, in order finally to land at a rotten peace.

[kap3:242]
So it went also with the pan-German movement, because it had not from the beginning laid the chief weight upon the winning of its followers from the circles of the broad mass. It became "bourgeois, genteel, damped radical."

[kap3:243]
Out of this error there grew for it, however, the second cause of the swift downfall.

[kap3:244]
The question in Austria for the Germandom was, at the time of the appearance of the pan-German movement, already desperate. From year to year the Parliament had become more an institution of the slow annihilation of the German people. Every attempt at a rescue in the twelfth hour could offer a, if also small, prospect of success only in the removal of this institution. Therewith there stepped toward the movement a question of principled significance:

[kap3:245]
Should one, in order to annihilate the Parliament, go into the Parliament, in order to "hollow out" the same, as one was wont to express oneself, "from inside out," or should one lead this fight from outside, attack-wise, against this institution in and for itself?

[kap3:246]
One went in and came out beaten.

[kap3:247]
To be sure, one had to go in.

[kap3:248]
To carry through the fight against such a might from outside means to arm oneself with unshakeable courage, but also to be ready for endless sacrifices. One attacks the bull therewith at the horns and will receive many heavy thrusts, will sometimes plunge to the ground, in order perhaps once to be able to raise oneself again only with broken limbs, and only after the heaviest

[kap3:249]
fighting will the victory turn itself toward the bold attacker. Only the greatness of the sacrifices will win new fighters of the cause, until finally to persistence comes the wage of success.

[kap3:250]
For this, however, one needs the children of the people out of the broad masses.

[kap3:251]
They alone are resolved and tenacious enough to fight this strife through to the bloody end.

[kap3:252]
Pan-Germans and Parliament

[kap3:253]
But this broad mass the pan-German movement precisely did not possess; so there remained to it also nothing else over than to go into the Parliament.

[kap3:254]
It would be false to believe that this resolution was the result of long inner soul-torments or even only of considerations; no, one thought of nothing else at all. The participation in this nonsense was only the precipitate of general, unclear conceptions of the significance and the effect of such an own participation in the institution, in principle indeed already recognized as false. In general one probably hoped for oneself an easing of the enlightenment of broader masses of people, in that one now got opportunity to speak before the "forum of the whole nation." Also it seemed to illumine that the attack at the root of the evil must be more successful than the storming from outside. Through the protection of immunity one believed the security of the individual front-fighter strengthened, so that the force of the attack could only heighten itself thereby.

[kap3:255]
In reality, however, things came essentially otherwise.

[kap3:256]
The forum before which the pan-German deputies spoke had not become greater, but rather smaller; for everyone speaks only before the circle which is able to hear him, or which through the reports of the press receives a rendering of the spoken.

[kap3:257]
The greatest immediate forum of hearers, however, is represented not by the lecture hall of a parliament, but by the great public people's assembly.

[kap3:258]
For in it there find themselves thousands of men who have only come in order to take in what the speaker has to say to them, while in the sitting hall of the House of Deputies there are only a few hundred, mostly also only there in order to take the diets into receipt, by no means in order perhaps to let the wisdom of the one or the other Mr. "representative of the people" illumine into themselves.

[kap3:259]
Above all, however: It is indeed always the same public, which will never more learn anything in addition, since to it, besides the understanding, indeed also the will needed hereto, if also ever so modest, is lacking.

[kap3:260]
Never will one of these representatives of the people of himself give the honour to the better truth, in order then also to place himself in its service. No, this not a single one will do, unless he has ground to hope, through such a turn, to be able still to save his mandate for a further session. Only

[kap3:261]
therefore, when it lies in the air that the hitherto party at a coming election will cut off badly, will these ornaments of manliness set out on the way and see whether and how they might come to the other, presumably better-cutting-off party or direction, whereby this change of position, to be sure, is wont to proceed under a cloudburst of moral groundings. Therefore there will always, when a standing party appears to have fallen to the disfavour of the people in so great an extent that the probability of an annihilating defeat threatens, begin a great wandering: the parliamentary rats leave the party ship.

[kap3:262]
But with better knowledge or willing this has nothing to do, but only with that clairvoyant giftedness which warns such a parliament-bedbug just still at the right time and so lets it fall ever again upon another warm party bed.

[kap3:263]
To speak before such a "forum" really means, however, to throw pearls before the well-known animals. That is truly not worth it! The success here cannot be otherwise than zero.

[kap3:264]
And so it was also. The pan-German deputies might talk their throats hoarse: the effect remained completely out.

[kap3:265]
But the press either silenced them dead or tore their speeches so that every coherence, indeed often even the sense was twisted or wholly lost, and thereby the public opinion received only a very bad picture of the intentions of the new movement. It was quite without significance what the individual gentlemen spoke; the significance lay in that which one got to read of them. This, however, was an extract from their speeches which in its tornness could only work nonsensically and — was to. Thereby, however, the only forum before which they now in truth spoke consisted of scarcely five hundred parliamentarians, and this says enough.

[kap3:266]
The worst, however, was the following:

[kap3:267]
The pan-German movement could only then reckon on success if it grasped from the first day that it was here not allowed to be a matter of a new party, but rather of a new world-view. Only such a one alone was able to bring up the inner force to fight out this gigantic fight. For this, however, as leaders only the very best and also most courageous heads are fit, now once.

[kap3:268]
If the fight for a world-view is not led by heroes ready for sacrifice, there will in a short time also no more death-courageous fighters find themselves. Whoever here fights for his own existence can for the generality no longer have much left over.

[kap3:269]
In order, however, to preserve this presupposition for oneself, it is necessary for everyone to know that the new movement can offer honour and fame before posterity, in the present, however, nothing. The more a movement has to give away of easily-to-be-won posts and places, the greater will be the rush of inferiors, until finally these political occasion-workers overgrow a successful party in such number that the honest fighter of once no longer recognizes the old movement at all and the newly-come-to-it decisively reject him himself as a bothersome "uncalled one." Therewith, however, the "mission" of such a movement is settled.

[kap3:270]
As soon as the pan-German movement gave itself over to the Parliament, it received indeed also "parliamentarians" instead of leaders and fighters. It sank therewith onto the level of one of the ordinary political day-parties and lost the force to oppose a doomful fate with the defiance of martyrdom. Instead of fighting, it now learned also to "speak" and to "negotiate." But the new parliamentarian already in a short time felt it as a finer, because more riskless, duty to fight out the new world-view with the "spiritual" weapons of parliamentary eloquence than to plunge himself, if needed, under stake of his own life into a fight whose outcome was uncertain, on all cases, however, could bring in nothing.

[kap3:271]
Since one now once sat in the Parliament, the followers outside began to hope and to wait for wonders, which naturally did not enter and also could not enter at all. One therefore became already in a short time impatient; for also that which one so got to hear of one's own deputies corresponded in no manner to the expectations of the electors. This was easily explicable, since the hostile press indeed guarded itself against conveying a truthful picture of the working of the pan-German representatives to the people.

[kap3:272]
The more, however, the new representatives of the people received taste for the yet somewhat milder kind of "revolutionary" fight in parliament and diets, the less they found themselves still ready to return into the more dangerous enlightenment work of the broad strata of the people.

[kap3:273]
The Significance of the Speech

[kap3:274]
The mass assembly, the only way of a really effective, because immediately personal influencing and thereby alone possible winning of great parts of the people, was therefore ever more put back.

[kap3:275]
As soon as the beer table of the assembly hall was definitively exchanged with the rostrum of the Parliament, in order from this forum to pour the speeches instead into the people into the heads of its so-called "Chosen," the pan-German movement also ceased to be a people's movement and sank in a short time together into a more or less earnestly to be taken club of academic discussions.

[kap3:276]
The bad impression conveyed through the press was accordingly in no manner more corrected by personal assembly activity of the individual gentlemen, so that finally the word "pan-German" received a very evil sound in the ears of the broad people.

[kap3:277]
For that may all the writing knights and fops of today especially let be said to themselves: the greatest overturnings on this world have never been led by a goose-quill!

[kap3:278]
No, to the pen it always only remained reserved to ground them theoretically. But the might which brought the great historical avalanches of religious and political kind into rolling was since primeval times only the magic force of the spoken word.

[kap3:279]
The broad mass of a people above all is subject always only to the force of speech. But all great movements are people's movements, are volcano outbreaks of human passions and soul-feelings, stirred up either by the cruel goddess of need or by the firebrand of the word hurled among the mass, and are not lemonade-like effusions of aestheticizing littérateurs and salon heroes.

[kap3:280]
Effect on the Mass

[kap3:281]
Fates of peoples only a storm of hot passion is able to turn; passion, however, only he can awaken who himself carries it in his interior.

[kap3:282]
It alone then presents to the one chosen by it the words which, similar to hammer-blows, are able to open the doors to the heart of a people.

[kap3:283]
To whom, however, passion is denied and the mouth remains closed, him heaven has not chosen as proclaimer of its will.

[kap3:284]
Therefore may every writer remain at his inkwell, in order to be active "theoretically," if understanding and capability suffice for this; as leader, however, he is neither born nor chosen.

[kap3:285]
A movement with great goals must therefore anxiously be at pains not to lose the connection with the broad people.

[kap3:286]
It has to examine every question in the first line from this point of view and to meet its decision in this direction.

[kap3:287]
It must further avoid everything which could diminish or even only weaken its capability to work upon the mass, not perhaps out of "demagogic" grounds, no, but out of the simple knowledge that without the mighty force of the mass of a people no great idea, may it also appear ever so noble and high, is to be realized.

[kap3:288]
The hard reality alone must determine the way to the goal; not to want to go unpleasant ways means on this world only too often to renounce the goal; one may then want this or not.

[kap3:289]
As soon as the pan-German movement, through its parliamentary adjustment, displaced the chief weight of its activity instead into the people into the Parliament, it lost the future and won for it cheap successes of the moment.

[kap3:290]
It chose the easier fight and was therewith, however, no longer worth the last victory.

[kap3:291]
I have precisely thought these questions through already in Vienna most thoroughly and seen in their not-recognition one of the chief causes of the collapse of the movement which in my eyes was then called to take the leadership of the Germandom into its hand.

[kap3:292]
The two first errors which let the pan-German movement fail stood in a related relation to one another. The lacking knowledge of the inner driving forces of great overturnings led to an insufficient estimation of the significance of the broad masses of the people;

[kap3:293]
out of this there resulted the low interest in the social question, the deficient and insufficient wooing for the soul of the lower strata of the nation, as well as also the adjustment to the Parliament, only favouring this.

[kap3:294]
Had one recognized the unheard-of might which devolves to the mass as beareress of revolutionary resistance at all times, then one would have worked otherwise in social as in propagandistic direction. Then also the chief weight of the movement would not have been laid into the Parliament, but upon workshop and street.

[kap3:295]
But also the third error carries the last germ in the not-recognition of the worth of the mass, which, through superior spirits once set in motion in a definite direction, then, however, also, similar to a flywheel, gives to the strength of the attack weight and uniform persistence.

[kap3:296]
The Away-from-Rome Movement

[kap3:297]
The heavy fight which the pan-German movement fought out with the Catholic Church is only explicable out of the insufficient understanding that one was able to bring toward the soul-disposition of the people.

[kap3:298]
The causes of the fierce attack of the new party against Rome lay in the following:

[kap3:299]
As soon as the House of Habsburg had finally resolved to reshape Austria into a Slavic state, one seized to every means which in this direction appeared somehow suitable. Also religious institutions were by this most conscienceless ruling house unscrupulously placed into the service of the new "state idea."

[kap3:300]
The employment of Czech parishes and their spiritual soul-carers was only one of the many means in order to come to this goal, a general Slavisation of Austria.

[kap3:301]
The process played itself out approximately as follows:

[kap3:302]
In purely German communities Czech pastors were set in, who slowly but surely began to place the interests of the Czech people above the interests of the Church and became germ-cells of the de-Germanization process.

[kap3:303]
The German clergy failed against such a procedure unfortunately almost completely. Not only that it itself was completely unusable for a similar fight in the German sense, it was also not able to meet the attacks of the others with the needed resistance. So the Germandom, over the detour of confessional misuse on the one side and through insufficient defence on the other, was slowly but incessantly pushed back.

[kap3:304]
If this in the small took place as set forth, then unfortunately the relations in the large lay not much otherwise.

[kap3:305]
Also here the anti-German attempts of the Habsburgs experienced, through the higher clergy above all, not the commanded defence, while the representation of the German interests itself stepped completely into the background.

[kap3:306]
The general impression could not be otherwise than that here there lay present a gross violation of German rights by the Catholic clergy as such.

[kap3:307]
Therewith, however, the Church seemed precisely not to feel with the German people, but to place itself in unjust manner on the side of the enemies of the same. The root of the whole evil, however, lay, above all according to Schönerer's opinion, in the leadership of the Catholic Church, not situated in Germany, and in the hostility toward the concerns of our people-body, conditioned already alone thereby.

[kap3:308]
The so-called cultural problems stepped thereby, as then almost with everything in Austria, nearly entirely into the background. Decisive for the adjustment of the pan-German movement toward the Catholic Church was much less the attitude of the same toward, say, science, etc., than rather its insufficient representation of German rights and conversely lasting furtherance of especially Slavic presumption and covetousness.

[kap3:309]
Georg Schönerer was now not the man to do a thing half. He took up the fight against the Church in the conviction of being able to save the German people only through it alone. The "Away-from-Rome Movement" seemed the mightiest, but to be sure also the heaviest attack-procedure which had to shatter the hostile stronghold. If it was successful, then also the unsalutary church-split in Germany was overcome, and the inner force of the Reich and of the German nation could through such a victory only gain in the most monstrous manner.

[kap3:310]
Alone neither the presupposition nor the conclusion of this fight was right.

[kap3:311]
Without doubt the national resistance force of the Catholic clergy of German nationality in all questions concerning the Germandom was lower than that of its non-German, especially Czech, office-brothers.

[kap3:312]
Just as much only an ignorant could not see that to the German clergy an offensive representation of German interests almost never even occurred.

[kap3:313]
Alone just as much must everyone not blinded admit that this is in the first line to be ascribed to a circumstance under which we Germans all together have to suffer most heavily: it is this, our objectivity in the adjustment toward our people-body just as toward anything else.

[kap3:314]
Just as the Czech clergyman stood subjectively toward his people and only objectively toward the Church, so the German pastor was subjectively devoted to the Church and remained objective toward the nation. An appearance which we in a thousand other cases, to our misfortune, can observe just so.

[kap3:315]
This is by no means only a special heirloom of Catholicism, but eats into with us in a short time almost every, especially state or ideal, institution.

[kap3:316]
Let one only compare the position which, e.g., our officialdom takes toward the attempts of a national rebirth, with that which the officialdom of another people would take in such a case. Or does one believe that the officer corps of the whole other world in a similar manner would put back the concerns of the nation under the phrase of "state authority," as this with us for five years is self-evident, indeed even still counts as especially meritorious? Do e.g. in the Jewish question not both confessions today take a standpoint which corresponds neither to the concerns of the nation nor to the real needs of religion? Let one indeed compare the attitude of a Jewish rabbi in all questions of only some significance for Jewry as race with the adjustment of the by far greatest part of our clergy, but please of both confessions!

[kap3:317]
We have this appearance always then, when it is a matter of the representation of an abstract idea in itself.

[kap3:318]
"State authority," "democracy," "pacifism," "international solidarity," etc., are nothing but concepts which with us become almost always such rigid, purely doctrinaire conceptions that every judgment of general national life-necessities takes place exclusively only more from their point of view.

[kap3:319]
This unsalutary kind of consideration of all concerns under the point of view of a once preconceived opinion kills every capacity to think oneself subjectively into a matter which objectively contradicts one's own doctrine, and leads in the end to a complete reversal of means and purpose. One will turn oneself against every attempt of a national elevation if this could take place only under preceding removal of a bad, pernicious regime, since this would indeed be an offence against "state authority"; "state authority," however, is not a means to the purpose, but rather in the eyes of such an objectivity-fanatic represents the purpose itself, which is sufficient to fill out his whole wretched life. So one would e.g. stem oneself with indignation against the attempt of a dictatorship, even if its bearer were a Frederick the Great and the momentary state-artists of a parliament majority only incapable dwarfs or even inferior subjects, because the law of democracy to such a

[kap3:320]
blockhead of principle appears even more holy than the welfare of a nation. So the one will protect the worst tyranny, which ruins a people, since "state authority" embodies itself at the moment in it, while the other rejects even the most salutary government as soon as it does not correspond to his conception of "democracy."

[kap3:321]
Exactly so will our German pacifist, at every even most bloody violation of the nation — it may calmly go out from the worst military powers — be silent, if a change of this lot would be to be reached only through resistance, therefore force, for this would indeed contradict the spirit of his peace society. But the international German Socialist can be plundered by the other world in solidarity; he himself receipts it with brotherly affection and thinks not of retribution or even only remonstrance, because he is just — a German.

[kap3:322]
This may be sad, but to want to change a thing means to have to recognize it before.

[kap3:323]
Just so it behaves itself with the feeble representation of German concerns by a part of the clergy.

[kap3:324]
This is neither malicious, bad will in itself, nor conditioned by, say, commands from "above," but we see in such a deficient national resoluteness only the results of a just as deficient education to Germandom from youth on, as on the other hand, however, of a restless subjection under the idea become idol.

[kap3:325]
The education to democracy, to socialism of international kind, to pacifism, etc., is such a rigid and exclusive one, hence, regarded from them out, purely subjective, that therewith also the general picture of the rest of the world is influenced under this basic conception, while the position toward Germandom indeed from youth on was only a very objective one. So the pacifist, in that he gives himself subjectively and restlessly to his idea, at every even most unjust and heavy threatening of his people (so far as he is just a German) will always first seek after the objective right and never out of pure instinct of self-preservation place himself into the row of his herd and fight along.

[kap3:326]
How much this also holds for the individual confessions may the following still show:

[kap3:327]
Protestantism represents of itself the concerns of the Germandom better, so far as this in its birth and later tradition is grounded already at large

[kap3:328]
in it; it fails, however, in the moment when this defence of national interests would have to take place on a domain which, in the general line of its world of conception and traditional development, either is lacking or is even rejected out of some ground.

[kap3:329]
So Protestantism will always step in for the furtherance of all Germandom in itself, as soon as it is a matter of things of inner cleanliness or also national deepening, of the defence of German essence, German language, and also German freedom, since this all indeed lies firmly grounded in itself; it fights, however, at once in the most hostile manner every attempt to save the nation out of the clutches of its deadliest enemy, since its position toward Jewry now once is more or less firmly dogmatically laid down. Thereby, however, it turns here around the question without whose solution all other attempts of a German rebirth or an elevation remain completely senseless and impossible and stay so.

[kap3:330]
I possessed in my Vienna time leisure and opportunity enough also to examine this question unprejudiced, and could thereby still in daily intercourse establish the correctness of this outlook thousandfold.

[kap3:331]
In this focal point of the most different nationalities it showed itself at once most clearly that indeed only the German pacifist tries to regard the concerns of his own nation always objectively, but never the Jew perhaps those of the Jewish people; that only the German Socialist is "inter-national" in a sense which then forbids him to beg for his own people justice otherwise than through whining and wailing with the international comrades, never, however, also the Czech or Pole, etc.; in short, I already then recognized that the misfortune lies only partly in these doctrines in themselves, for the other part, however, in our completely insufficient education to our own people-body at large and in a lesser devotion to the same conditioned thereby.

[kap3:332]
Therewith the first purely theoretical grounding of the fight of the pan-German movement against Catholicism in itself fell away.

[kap3:333]
Let one educate the German people already from youth on with that exclusive recognition of the rights of its own people-body, and let one not poison already the children's hearts with the curse of our "objectivity," also in things of the preservation of one's own ego, then it will show itself in a short time that (a then, however, also radical national government presupposed) just as in

[kap3:334]
Ireland, Poland, or France, also in Germany the Catholic will always be a German.

[kap3:335]
But the mightiest proof for this has that time delivered which, for the last time, let our people step before the judgment-seat of history for the protection of its existence, to its fight on life and death.

[kap3:336]
So long as the leadership then from above did not lack, the people has fulfilled its duty and obligation in the most overwhelming manner. Whether Protestant pastor or Catholic priest, they both together contributed infinitely to the so long preservation of our resistance force, not only at the front, but still more at home. In these years, and especially in the first up-flaming, there was really in both camps only a single holy German Reich, for whose existence and future everyone just turned to his heaven.

[kap3:337]
One question the pan-German movement in Austria should once have laid before itself: Is the preservation of the Austrian Germandom under a Catholic faith possible or not? If yes, then the political party was not allowed to trouble itself about religious or even confessional things; if, however, no, then a religious reformation had to set in and never a political party.

[kap3:338]
Whoever over the detour of a political organization believes to be able to come to a religious reformation shows only that to him also every glimmer of the becoming of religious conceptions or even doctrines of faith and their churchly effects is lacking.

[kap3:339]
One really cannot serve two masters here. Whereby I hold the founding or destruction of a religion indeed as essentially greater than the founding or destruction of a state, let alone of a party.

[kap3:340]
Let one indeed not say that said attacks were only the defence against attacks of the other side!

[kap3:341]
Surely at all times conscienceless fellows have not shied from making also religion the instrument of their political businesses (for with such fellows it is almost always and exclusively a matter of this); alone just as surely it is false to make religion or also the confession responsible for a number of scoundrels who misuse it just as they would otherwise probably place anything else into the service of their lower instincts.

[kap3:342]
Nothing can be more fitting to such a parliamentary good-for-nothing and idler than when to him so opportunity is offered, at least afterward

[kap3:343]
still to attain the justification for his political shoving. For as soon as one makes religion or also the confession responsible for one's personal badness and therefore attacks it, the lying fellow at once calls, under enormous screaming, all the world to witness how justified his proceeding hitherto was, and how only to him and his mouth-fertility alone the rescue of religion and Church is to be thanked. The just as stupid as forgetful contemporary world then recognizes the true originator of the whole fight, already for the sake of the great screaming, mostly not, or remembers him no more, and the scoundrel has indeed now actually reached his goal.

[kap3:344]
That this has nothing to do with religion, such a cunning fox knows quite exactly; he will therefore so much the more laugh in secret into his little fist, while his honest but unskilful opponent loses the game, in order one day, despairing of the loyalty and faith of humanity, to withdraw himself from everything.

[kap3:345]
But it would also in another respect be only unjust to make religion as such or even the Church responsible for the offences of individuals.

[kap3:346]
Let one compare the greatness of the visible organization standing before the eye with the average faulty-ness of men in general, and one will have to admit that the relation of good and bad is thereby better than probably anywhere else. Surely there are also among the priests themselves such to whom their holy office is only a means for the satisfaction of their political ambition, indeed who in the political fight in often more than deplorable manner forget that they should indeed be the guardians of a higher truth and not representatives of lie and defamation — alone to one such unworthy there meet then also again a thousand and more honourable soul-carers, most faithfully devoted to their mission, who in our today's just as mendacious as depraved time tower up like small islands out of a general swamp.

[kap3:347]
As little as I condemn and may condemn the Church as such when once a depraved subject in priest's frock offends in dirty manner against morality, so little, however, also when another among the many befouls and betrays his people-body, in courses of time in which this anyway is downright everyday. Especially today may one then not forget that to one such Ephialtes there also meet thousands who, with bleeding heart, feel along the misfortune of their people and just as the best of our nation long for the hour in which also to us heaven will once again smile.

[kap3:348]
Whoever, however, gives as answer that it is here not a matter of such small problems of the everyday, but of questions of principled truthfulness or dogmatic content at large, to him one can only give the needed answer with another question:

[kap3:349]
Do you believe yourself chosen by fate here to proclaim the truth, then do it; but have then also the courage not to want to do this over the detour of a political party — for this is also a shoving — but place precisely in the place of the worse of now your better of the future. If the courage for this is lacking to you, or if your better is itself not quite clear to you, then leave the fingers from it; on all cases, however, do not try to sneak, over the detour of a political movement, what you do not trust yourself to do with open visor.

[kap3:350]
Political parties have nothing to do with religious problems, so long as these do not, as foreign to the people, undermine the custom and morality of one's own race; just as religion is not to be mixed up with political party mischief.

[kap3:351]
If churchly dignitaries make use of religious institutions or also doctrines in order to damage their people-body, one may never follow them on this way and fight with equal weapons.

[kap3:352]
To the political leader religious doctrines and institutions of his people must always be unassailable, otherwise he may not be a politician, but shall become a reformer, if he possesses the stuff for this!

[kap3:353]
Another attitude would above all in Germany lead to a catastrophe.

[kap3:354]
At the study of the pan-German movement and its fight against Rome I arrived then, and especially in the course of later years, at the following conviction: The low understanding of this movement for the significance of the social problem cost it the truly fight-capable mass of the people; the going into the Parliament took from it the mighty swing and burdened it with all the weaknesses proper to this institution; the fight against the Catholic Church made it impossible in numerous small and middle circles and robbed it thereby of countless of the best elements which the nation at large can call its own.

[kap3:355]
The practical result of the Austrian culture-fight was almost equal to zero.

[kap3:356]
Indeed it succeeded in tearing away against a hundred thousand members from the Church, alone without this thereby suffering even a special damage. It truly needed to weep no tear after the lost "little sheep" in this case; for it lost only what already long inwardly no longer fully belonged to it before. This was the distinction of the new reformation over against the one of once: that once many of the best of the Church turned themselves from it out of inner religious conviction, while now only the anyway lukewarm went, and indeed out of "considerations" of political nature.

[kap3:357]
Precisely from the political point of view, however, was the result just as ridiculous as, however, again sad.

[kap3:358]
Again a success-promising political salvation-movement of the German nation had perished, because it was not led with the needed ruthless sobriety, but lost itself on domains which could only lead to a splitting-up.

[kap3:359]
For one thing is surely true:

[kap3:360]
The pan-German movement would probably never have made this error if it had not possessed too little understanding for the psyche of the broad mass. Had it been known to its leaders that, in order to be able to wrest successes at all, already out of purely soul considerations one may never show the mass two and more opponents, since this otherwise leads to a complete splitting of the fighting force, then already out of this ground the direction of thrust of the pan-German movement would have been adjusted only upon one opponent alone. There is nothing more dangerous for a political party than when it lets itself be led in its resolutions by those steam-scamps in all alleys who want everything without ever being able to achieve even the least.

[kap3:361]
Also if at the individual confession ever so much would really be to find fault with, the political party may yet not for a moment lose the fact from the eye that according to all hitherto experience of history it had never yet succeeded to a purely political party in similar situations to come to a religious reformation. One does not, however, study history in order then, when it should come to practical application, not to recall its doctrines or to believe that now things just lay otherwise, hence its primeval truths would no longer be to be applied, but one learns out of it precisely the useful application for the present. Whoever does not bring this about should not imagine himself to be a political leader; he is

[kap3:362]
in truth a shallow, if also mostly very conceited drip, and all good will does not excuse his practical incapability.

[kap3:363]
Concentration upon One Opponent

[kap3:364]
At large the art of all truly great people's leaders at all times consists in the first line therein, not to split up the attention of a people, but always to concentrate it upon a single opponent. The more unitary this stake of the fighting-will of a people takes place, the greater will be the magnetic power of attraction of a movement, and the mightier the weight of the thrust. It belongs to the geniality of a great leader to let even apart-lying opponents always appear as belonging only to one category, because the knowledge of different enemies with weakish and unsure characters leads only too easily to the beginning of doubt in one's own right.

[kap3:365]
As soon as the wavering mass sees itself in the fight against too many enemies, objectivity will at once set itself in and throw up the question whether really all others are in the wrong and only one's own people or one's own movement alone find themselves in the right.

[kap3:366]
Therewith, however, comes also already the first paralysis of one's own force. Therefore a multiplicity of inwardly different opponents must always be grasped together, so that in the insight of the mass of one's own followers the fight is led only against one enemy alone. This strengthens the belief in one's own right and heightens the embitterment against the attacker upon the same.

[kap3:367]
That the pan-German movement of once did not grasp this cost it the success.

[kap3:368]
Its goal was rightly seen, the willing pure, but the way struck was false. It resembled a mountain-climber who indeed keeps the summit to be climbed in the eye, also with greatest resoluteness and force sets himself on the way, alone bestows no attention upon this itself, but, always the glance directed upon the goal, neither sees nor tests the constitution of the ascent and finally fails at it.

[kap3:369]
The Way of the Christian-Socials

[kap3:370]
Conversely the relation with the great rivaless, the Christian-Social Party, seemed to lie.

[kap3:371]
The way which it struck was cleverly and rightly chosen; alone there lacked the clear knowledge of the goal.

[kap3:372]
In almost all concerns in which the pan-German movement failed, the adjustment of the Christian-Social Party was right and full of plan.

[kap3:373]
It possessed the needed understanding for the significance of the mass and secured for itself at least a part of the same through evident stressing of its social character from the first day on. In that it adjusted itself in essential manner upon the winning of the small and lower middle and craftsman estate, it received a just as faithful as enduring and sacrifice-ready following. It avoided every fight against a religious institution and secured for itself thereby the support of so mighty an organization as the Church now once represents. It possessed accordingly also only a single truly great chief opponent. It recognized the worth of a generous propaganda and was virtuosa in working upon the soul instincts of the broad mass of its followers.

[kap3:374]
That also it nevertheless was not able to reach the dreamed goal of a rescue of Austria lay in two defects of its way as well as in the unclearness over the goal itself.

[kap3:375]
Fight against the Jews on a Religious Foundation

[kap3:376]
The antisemitism of the new movement was built up instead upon racial knowledge upon religious conception.

[kap3:377]
The ground why this error slipped under was the same which also occasioned the second mistake.

[kap3:378]
If the Christian-Social Party wanted to save Austria, then it was not allowed, according to the opinion of its founders, to place itself upon the standpoint of the race principle, since otherwise in a short time a general dissolution of the state had to enter. But especially the situation in Vienna itself demanded, according to the view of the leaders of the party, a maximally great leaving-aside of all separating moments and in their place a bringing-forward of all uniting points of view.

[kap3:379]
Vienna was at this time already so strongly permeated, especially with Czech elements, that only the greatest tolerance in respect of all race problems was able to hold these still in a party not from the outset German-hostile. If one wanted to save Austria, one was not allowed to renounce them. So one tried to win the especially very numerous Czech small-tradesmen in Vienna through the fight against the liberal Manchesterdom and believed thereby to have found, in the fight against Jewry on a religious foundation, a parole leading over all the people-differences of old Austria.

[kap3:380]
That such a fighting on such a foundation prepared the Jewishness only limited care lies on the hand. In the worst case a gush of baptismal water always still saved business and Jewry at once.

[kap3:381]
With such a superficial grounding one also never came to an earnest scientific treatment of the whole problem and thereby only pushed back too many to whom this kind of antisemitism had to be incomprehensible at large. The wooing force of the idea was therewith almost exclusively bound to spiritually limited circles, if one did not want to come away from purely feeling-like sensation to a real knowledge. The intelligence behaved itself principally rejecting. The thing received so more and more the stamp as if it were in the whole affair only a matter of the

[kap3:382]
attempt of a new conversion of Jews or even of the expression of a certain competitive envy. Therewith, however, the fight lost the mark of a

[kap3:383]
inner and higher consecration and appeared to many, and not precisely the worst, as immoral and reprehensible. There lacked the conviction that it was here a matter of a life-question of the whole of humanity, of whose solution the fate of all non-Jewish peoples depends.

[kap3:384]
At this halfness the worth of the antisemitic adjustment of the Christian-Social Party went lost.

[kap3:385]
It was a sham-antisemitism which was almost worse than none at all; for so one was lulled into security, believed to have the opponent at the ears, was, however, oneself led by the nose.

[kap3:386]
The Jew, however, had already in a short time accustomed himself also to this kind of antisemitism so that its falling-away would surely have missed him more than its presence hindered him.

[kap3:387]
Had one here already to bring to the nationality state a heavy sacrifice, so still much more to the representation of the Germandom in itself.

[kap3:388]
Man was not allowed to be "nationalistic," if one did not want to lose the ground under the feet in Vienna itself. One hoped through a gentle going-around of this question still to save the Habsburg state and drove it precisely thereby into the doom. The movement, however, lost therewith the mighty force-source which alone in the length is able to fill up a political party with inner driving force. The Christian-Social movement precisely thereby became a party like just every other also.

[kap3:389]
Pan-Germans and Christian-Socials

[kap3:390]
I have once followed both movements most attentively, the one out of the pulse-beat of the inner heart out, the other, carried away by admiration for the rare man who already then appeared to me as a bitter symbol of the whole Austrian Germandom.

[kap3:391]
When the mighty funeral procession drove the dead mayor from the town hall away toward the Ringstraße, I also found myself among the many hundreds of thousands who looked on at the tragedy. In inner emotion feeling told me thereby that also the work of this man would have to be in vain through the doom which would inevitably lead this state toward the downfall. Had Dr. Karl Lueger lived in Germany, he would have been placed into the row of the great heads of our people; that he worked in this impossible state was the misfortune of his work and of himself. When he died, the little flames on the Balkans already twitched forth from month to month more greedily, so that fate graciously spared him to see what he still believed to be able to prevent.

[kap3:392]
I, however, tried to find out from the failure of the one movement and the ill-success of the second the causes, and came to the sure conviction that, quite apart from the impossibility of still attaining a consolidation of the state in old Austria, the errors of the two parties were the following:

[kap3:393]
The pan-German movement had indeed right in its principled view of the goal of a German renewal, but was unhappy in the choice of the way. It was nationalistic, alone unfortunately not social enough to win the mass. Its antisemitism, however, rested upon the right knowledge of the significance of the race problem and not upon religious conceptions. Its fight against a definite confession, against it, was actually and tactically false.

[kap3:394]
The Christian-Social movement possessed an unclear conception of the goal of a German rebirth, but had understanding and luck at the seeking of its ways as a party. It grasped the significance of the social question, erred in its fight against Jewry, and possessed no inkling of the might of the national thought.

[kap3:395]
Had the Christian-Social Party, to its clever knowledge of the broad mass, still possessed the right conception of the significance of the race problem, as this

[kap3:396]
the pan-German movement had grasped, and had she herself finally been nationalistic, or would the pan-German movement, to its right knowledge of the goal of the Jewish question and of the significance of the national thought, still have taken on the practical cleverness of the Christian-Social Party, especially, however, its adjustment to socialism, then this would have yielded that movement which already then, according to my conviction, could have intervened with success into the German fate.

[kap3:397]
Growing Aversion against the Habsburg State

[kap3:398]
That this was not so lay for by far the greatest part, however, in the essence of the Austrian state.

[kap3:399]
Since I saw my conviction realized in no other party, I could in the sequel also no longer resolve to enter into one of the standing organizations or even to fight along. I held already then all of the political movements for failed and for incapable of carrying through a national rebirth of the German people in a greater and not outward extent.

[kap3:400]
But my inner aversion against the Habsburg state grew in this time ever more.

[kap3:401]
The more I began especially also to busy myself with foreign-political questions, the more my conviction won ground that this state structure would have to become only the misfortune of the Germandom. Ever more clearly I finally also saw that the fate of the German nation would no longer be decided from this place out, but in the Reich itself. This held, however, not only for general political questions, but not less also for all appearances of the whole cultural life at large.

[kap3:402]
The Austrian state showed also here, on the domain of purely cultural or artistic affairs, all the marks of exhaustion, at least, however, of meaninglessness for the German nation. Most of all this held for the domain of architecture. The newer architecture could already therefore in Austria not come to especially great successes, because the tasks since the building-out of the Ringstraße, at least in Vienna, were only more insignificant as against the plans rising up in Germany.

[kap3:403]
So I began ever more to lead a double life; understanding and reality bade me go through in Austria a just as bitter as salutary school, alone the heart dwelt elsewhere.

[kap3:404]
A pressing discontent had then taken possession of me, the more I recognized the inner hollowness of this state, the impossibility of still saving it, but thereby with all certainty felt that he in all and everything could only more represent the misfortune of the German people.

[kap3:405]
Austria — an Old Mosaic Picture

[kap3:406]
I was convinced that this state must just as much cramp and hinder every truly great German as it conversely would further every non-German appearance. Loathsome to me was the race-conglomerate which the Reich capital showed, loathsome this whole mixture of peoples of Czechs, Poles, Hungarians, Ruthenians, Serbs, and Croats, etc., between everything, however, as eternal fission-fungus of humanity — Jews and again Jews.

[kap3:407]
To me the giant city appeared as the embodiment of blood-disgrace.

[kap3:408]
My German of the youth time was the dialect which also Lower Bavaria speaks; I was able neither to forget it nor to learn the Vienna jargon. The longer I dwelt in this city, the more my hate against the foreign mixture of peoples rose, which began to gnaw away this old German culture place.

[kap3:409]
The thought, however, that this state would still be to hold for a longer time appeared to me downright ridiculous.

[kap3:410]
Austria was then like an old mosaic picture whose cement, which binds the individual little stones together, has become old and crumbly; so long as the work of art is not touched, it is still able to feign its existence further; as soon as it, however, receives a thrust, it breaks apart into a thousand little shards. The question was therefore only the one, when the thrust would come.

[kap3:411]
Since my heart never beat for an Austrian monarchy, but always only for a German Reich, the hour of the decay of this state could appear to me only as the beginning of the redemption of the German nation.

[kap3:412]
Out of all these grounds there arose ever stronger the longing finally to go there, whither since so early youth secret wishes and secret love drew me.

[kap3:413]
The School of My Life

[kap3:414]
I hoped one day as master-builder to make myself a name and so, in small or great frame, which fate then just would assign to me, to consecrate to the nation my honest service.

[kap3:415]
Finally, however, I wanted to become partaker of the happiness to be allowed to be and to work at the place from which once indeed also my most burning heart's wish had to go into fulfilment: the joining of my beloved homeland to the common fatherland, the German Reich.

[kap3:416]
Many will even today not be able to grasp the greatness of such a longing; alone I turn myself to those to whom fate has either hitherto refused this happiness or in cruel hardness taken it away again; I turn myself to all those who, loosed from the motherland, have themselves to fight for the holy good of the language, who on account of their disposition of loyalty toward the fatherland are persecuted and tormented, and who now in painful emotion long for the hour which lets them return again to the heart of the dear mother; I turn myself to all these and know: They will understand me!

[kap3:417]
Only whoever himself feels on his own body what it means to be German without being allowed to belong to the dear fatherland is able to measure the deep longing which at all times burns in the hearts of the children separated from the motherland. It torments those seized by it and refuses them contentment and happiness so long, until the gates of the father's house open themselves and in the common Reich the common blood finds peace and rest again. But Vienna was and remained for me the heaviest, if also most thorough school of my life. I had once entered this city still as a half boy and left it as a still and earnest become man. I received in it the foundations for a world-view in the large and a political manner of consideration in the small, which I later only still had to complete in the individual, which, however, never more left me. The right worth of the then apprenticeship years I am, to be sure, myself only today able fully to estimate. Therefore I have treated this time somewhat more extensively, since it gave me precisely in those questions the first instruction by spectacle which belong with to the foundations of the party which, arising out of smallest beginnings, is minded in the course of scarcely five years to develop itself into a great mass movement. I know not how my position toward Jewry, toward the

[kap3:418]
Social Democracy, better toward the whole of Marxism, toward the social question, etc., would be today, if not already a stock of personal outlooks in so early time through the pressure of fate — and through own learning had formed itself.

[kap3:419]
For, if also the misfortune of the fatherland is able to stimulate thousands and again thousands to thinking over the inner grounds of the collapse, so this can yet never lead to that thoroughness and deeper insight which discloses itself to him who himself first after years-long wrestling became master of fate.
