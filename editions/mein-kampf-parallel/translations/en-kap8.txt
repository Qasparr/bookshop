[kap8:0]
Still at the end of November 1918 I came back to Munich. I drove again to the replacement battalion of my regiment, which found itself in the hand of "soldiers' councils." The whole operation was so repulsive to me that I at once resolved, if possible, to go away again. With a faithful war comrade, Ernst Schmidt, I came to Traunstein and remained until the dissolution of the camp there.

[kap8:1]
In March 1919 we went back again to Munich.

[kap8:2]
The situation was untenable and pressed forcedly to a further continuation of the revolution. The death of Eisner only accelerated the development and led finally to the council dictatorship, better expressed: to a temporary Jew-dominion, as it originally floated before the eyes of the originators of the whole revolution as a goal.

[kap8:3]
In this time endless plans hunted one another in my head. For days I considered what one could at large only do; alone always the end of every consideration was the sober establishment that I as a nameless one did not possess even the least presupposition to any expedient acting. On the grounds why I also then could not resolve myself to go to one of the existing parties I shall still come to speak. In the course of the new council revolution I stepped forth for the first time in such a way that I drew upon myself the disfavour of the Central Council. On 27 April 1919 early in the morning I was to be arrested — the three fellows, however, faced with the held-out carbine, did not possess the necessary courage and drew off again as they had come.

[kap8:4]
Few days after the liberation of Munich I was detailed to the investigation commission over the revolution processes at the 2nd Infantry Regiment.

[kap8:5]
This was my first more or less purely political active activity.

[kap8:6]
Discussion of the Formation of a New Party

[kap8:7]
Already few weeks thereupon I received the order to take part in a "course" which was held for members of the armed force. In it the soldier should receive determinate foundations for civic thinking. For me the value of the whole arrangement lay therein, that I now received the possibility to get to know some like-minded comrades with whom I was able thoroughly to talk through the momentary situation. We were all more or less firmly convinced that Germany through the parties of the November crime, Centre and Social Democracy, would no more be rescued out of the ripening-up collapse, but that also the so-called "bourgeois-national" formations, even at best will, would never more understand how to make good what had happened. Here a whole series of presuppositions lacked, without which such a work just could not succeed. The following time has given right to our then view.

[kap8:8]
So then in our small circle the formation of a new party was discussed. The basic thoughts which floated before us thereby were the same which then later in the "German Workers' Party" came to realization. The name of the newly-to-be-founded movement had from the beginning to offer the possibility to come up to the broad mass; for without this property the whole work seemed purposeless and superfluous. So we came upon the name "Social-Revolutionary Party"; this therefore because indeed the social views of the new founding actually meant a revolution.

[kap8:9]
The deeper ground hereto lay, however, in the following:

[kap8:10]
However much I also already earlier had occupied myself with economic problems, so it yet had more or less always remained within the borders which resulted out of the consideration of the social questions in themselves. Only later this frame widened in consequence of the examination of German alliance politics. It was indeed to a very great part the result of a false estimation of the economy as well as of the unclarity over the possible foundations of a nourishment of the German people in the future. All these thoughts, however, still rested upon the opinion that capital in every case would only be the result of work and hence, like this itself, would be subjected to the correction of all those factors which are able either to further or to check human activity. Therein would then also lie the national significance of capital, that it itself so completely from greatness, freedom, and might of the state,

[kap8:11]
therefore of the nation, depends, that this boundness alone already would have to lead to a furthering of the state and of the nation on the part of this capital, out of the simple urge of self-preservation, respectively of further increase. This being-referred of capital to the independent free state would therefore on its side force this to step in for this freedom, might, strength, etc., of the nation.

[kap8:12]
The Two Kinds of Capital

[kap8:13]
Therewith also the task of the state over against capital was a relatively simple and clear one: it had only to care for it that it remained servant of the state and did not imagine itself mistress of the nation. This taking of position could then hold itself in two border-lines: preservation of a living-capable national and independent economy on the one side, securing of the social rights of the wage-earners on the other.

[kap8:14]
The difference of this pure capital as last result of the creating work over against a capital whose existence and essence rests exclusively on speculation I was earlier not yet able to recognize with the desirable clarity. There lacked to me hereto the first suggestion, which just did not come up to me.

[kap8:15]
This was now in the most thorough manner seen to by one of the various gentlemen lecturing in the already mentioned courses: Gottfried Feder.

[kap8:16]
For the first time in my life I perceived a principled confrontation with the international stock-exchange and loan capital. After I had heard Feder's first lecture, there also at once twitched through my head the thought now to have found the way to one of the most essential presuppositions for the founding of a new party.

[kap8:17]
The merit of Feder rested in my eyes therein, with ruthless brutality to have laid fast the just as speculative as national-economic character of stock-exchange and loan capital, but to have laid bare its age-eternal presupposition of interest. His executions were in all fundamental questions so right that the critics of the same from the outset disputed less the theoretical correctness of the idea than rather doubted the practical possibility of its carrying-out. Alone, what so in the eyes of others was a weakness of the Federian presentations formed in mine their strength.

[kap8:18]
The Task of the Programmatist

[kap8:19]
The task of the programmatist is not to establish the various degrees of the fulfillability of a thing, but to lay clear the thing as such; that means: he has to care less about the way than the goal. Hereby, however, the principled correctness of an idea decides, and not the difficulty of its carrying-out. As soon as the programmatist tries, in place of absolute truth, to take account of the so-called "expedience" and "reality," his work will cease to be a polar star of seeking humanity, in order instead to become a recipe of the everyday. The programmatist of a movement has to lay fast the goal of the same; the politician to strive for its fulfilment. The one will accordingly in his thinking be determined by eternal truth, the other in his acting more by the respective practical reality. The greatness of the one lies in the absolute abstract correctness of his idea, that of the other in the right setting to the given facts and a useful application of the same, whereby to him as guiding star the goal of the programmatist has to serve. While as touchstone for the significance of a politician one may regard the success of his plans and deeds, that is therefore the becoming-reality of the same, the realization of the last intention of the programmatist can never take place, since indeed human thought is able to grasp truths, to set up crystal-clear goals; alone the restless fulfilment of the same will fail at the generally human incompleteness and insufficiency. The more abstractly right and therewith mightier the idea will be, so much the more impossible remains its complete fulfilment, so long as it now once depends on men. Therefore also the significance of the programmatist may not be measured at the fulfilment of his goals, but at the correctness of the same and the influence which they have taken on the development of humanity. Were it otherwise, the founders of religions would not be allowed to be reckoned to the greatest men on this earth, since indeed the fulfilment of their ethical intentions will never be one even only approximately complete. Even the religion of love is in its working only a weak reflection of the will of its sublime founder; alone its significance lies in the direction which it tried to give to a general human culture-, morality-, and morals-development.

[kap8:20]
Programmatist and Politician

[kap8:21]
The exceedingly great difference of the tasks of the programmatist and of the politician is also the cause why almost never a union of both in one person is to be found. It holds this especially of the so-called "successful" politician of small format, whose activity mostly really is only an "art of the possible," as Bismarck designated politics at large somewhat modestly. The freer such a "politician" holds himself from great ideas, the lighter and often also more visible, always, however, faster his successes will be. Freely, they are therewith also consecrated to earthly transitoriness and outlive sometimes not the death of their fathers. The work of such politicians is in the great and whole for the afterworld meaningless, since their successes in the present indeed only rest upon the keeping-away of all really great and incisive problems and thoughts, which as such also for the later generations would have been of value.

[kap8:22]
The carrying-out of such goals, which still for the farthest times have value and significance, is for the advocate of the same mostly little rewarding and finds only seldom understanding with the great mass, to whom beer- and milk-decrees first illuminate better than far-looking future plans, whose realization can only later enter, whose use, however, at large comes good only to the afterworld.

[kap8:23]
So already out of a certain vanity, which always is a relative of stupidity, the great mass of politicians will hold itself away from all really heavy future drafts, in order not to go lost of the moment-sympathy of the great heap. The success and the significance of such a politician then lie exclusively in the present and are for the afterworld not present. The small heads are wont also to be little bothered by this; they are content therewith.

[kap8:24]
Otherwise lie the relations with the programmatist. His significance lies almost always only in the future, since he indeed not seldom is that which one designates with the word "world-foreign." For if the art of the politician really counts as an art of the possible, then the programmatist belongs to those of whom it is said that they please the gods only when they demand and want impossibles. He will almost always have to do renunciation on the recognition of the present; harvests, however, therefor, in case his thoughts are immortal, the fame of the afterworld.

[kap8:25]
The Marathon Runners of History

[kap8:26]
Within long periods of humanity it can once happen that the politician marries himself with the programmatist. The more intimate, however, this fusion is, so much the greater are the resistances which stem themselves against the working of the politician. He works no more for requirements which illuminate every next-best philistine, but for goals which only the fewest comprehend. Therefore his life is then torn by love and hate. The protest of the present, which does not comprehend the man, wrestles with the recognition of the afterworld, for which he indeed also works.

[kap8:27]
For the greater the works of a man for the future are, the harder the present is able to grasp them; so much the harder is also the fight and so much the rarer the success. If it, however, nevertheless blooms in centuries to one, then perhaps in his late days already a soft shimmer of the coming fame can stream around him. Freely, these great ones are only the marathon runners of history; the laurel wreath of the present touches only more the temples of the dying hero.

[kap8:28]
To them, however, are to be reckoned the great fighters on this world who, not understood by the present, are nevertheless ready to fight through the strife for their idea and ideals. They are those who once will stand nearest to the heart of the people; it seems almost so as if every individual then felt the duty to make good at the past what the present once sinned at the great ones. Their life and working is followed in moving thankful admiration and is able, especially in gloomy days, to lift up again broken hearts and despairing souls.

[kap8:29]
Hereto belong, however, not only the really great statesmen, but also all other great reformers. Beside Frederick the Great stand here Martin Luther as well as Richard Wagner.

[kap8:30]
Fight Against International Finance Capital

[kap8:31]
When I heard Gottfried Feder's first lecture over the "breaking of interest-slavery," I knew at once that here it is a matter of a theoretical truth which would have to become of immense significance for the future of the German people. The sharp separation of stock-exchange capital from the national economy offered the possibility to step against the internationalization of the German economy without at the same time, with the fight against capital at large, to threaten the ground of an independent folkish self-preservation. The development of Germany stood to me already much too clearly before the eyes than that I would not have known that the heaviest fight had to be fought out no more against the hostile peoples, but against international capital. In Feder's lecture I sensed a mighty parole for this coming wrestling. And also here the later development proved how right our then feeling was. Today we are no more laughed at by the clever-heads of our bourgeois politicians; today even these see, so far as they are not conscious liars, that international stock-exchange capital was not only the greatest inciter to the war, but precisely now after the fight's ending omits nothing to transform peace into hell.

[kap8:32]
The fight against international finance and loan capital has become the most important programme point of the fight of the German nation for its economic independence and freedom.

[kap8:33]
As concerns, however, the objections of the so-called practitioners, the following can be answered to them: All fears over the frightful economic consequences of a carrying-out of the "breaking of interest-slavery" are superfluous; for first, the hitherto economic recipes have become very bad for the German people; the takings of position to the questions of national self-assertion remind us very strongly of the expert opinions of similar experts in earlier times, for example of the Bavarian medical college on the occasion of the question of the introduction of the railway. All fears of this illustrious corporation of then are later, as known, not entered; the travellers in the trains of the new "steam horse" did not become dizzy, the onlookers also not sick, and on the board fences, in order

[kap8:34]
to make the new arrangement invisible, one has renounced — only the board walls before the heads of all so-called "experts" remained preserved also to the afterworld.

[kap8:35]
Only One Doctrine: People and Fatherland

[kap8:36]
Second, however, one shall note to oneself the following: Every and also the best idea becomes a danger when it imagines itself to be an end in itself, in reality, however, represents only a means to such a one — for me, however, and all truthful National Socialists, there is only one doctrine: People and Fatherland.

[kap8:37]
For what we have to fight is the securing of the existence and the increase of our race and our people, the nourishment of its children and keeping-pure of the blood, the freedom and independence of the fatherland, in order that our people may be able to ripen toward the fulfilment of the mission also assigned to it by the Creator of the universe.

[kap8:38]
Every thought and every idea, every doctrine and all knowledge have to serve this purpose. From this point of view also everything is to be tested and according to its expedience to be used or rejected. So no theory can freeze stiff to a deadly doctrine, since everything indeed has only to serve life.

[kap8:39]
So the knowledges of Gottfried Feder were the occasion to occupy myself in thorough manner with this domain, to me indeed up to then still little familiar, at large.

[kap8:40]
I began again to learn and came only now rightly to the understanding of the content of the will of the life-work of the Jew Karl Marx. His "Capital" now first became rightly understandable to me, just as the fight of the Social Democracy against the national economy, which only has to prepare the ground for the dominion of the really international finance and stock-exchange capital.

[kap8:41]
The "Education Officer"

[kap8:42]
Alone still in another respect were these courses for me of greatest consequential effect.

[kap8:43]
I reported myself one day for speaking-out. One of the participants believed to have to break a lance for the Jews and began to defend them in longer executions. This irritated me to a reply. The by far overwhelming number of the course participants present placed themselves on my standpoint. The result, however, was that I few days later was determined thereto to march into a then Munich regiment as a so-called "education officer."

[kap8:44]
The discipline of the troop was at this time still fairly weak. It suffered under the after-effects of the soldiers'-council period. Only quite slowly and cautiously could one pass over thereto, in place of the "voluntary" obedience — as one under Kurt Eisner was wont so beautifully to designate the pigsty — again to introduce military discipline and subordination. Just so the troop itself should learn to feel and think nationally and fatherlandishly. In these two directions lay the domains of my new activity.

[kap8:45]
I began with all desire and love. Offered itself to me indeed now with one blow the opportunity to speak before a greater hearership; and what I earlier always, without knowing it, out of pure feeling had simply assumed, now entered: I could "speak." Also the voice had already become so much better that I at least in small team rooms everywhere remained sufficiently understandable.

[kap8:46]
No task could make me happier than this, for now I was able, still before my discharge, in the institution to perform useful services which had lain infinitely on my heart: in the army.

[kap8:47]
I was allowed also to speak of success: Many hundreds, indeed probably thousands of comrades I have in the course of my lectures led back again to their people and fatherland. I "nationalized" the troop and could on this way help along to strengthen the general discipline.

[kap8:48]
Again I thereby learned to know a number of like-minded comrades, who later began with to form the ground-stock of the new movement.
