In the spring of 1912 I came definitively to Munich.

[kap4:1]
The city itself was so well known to me as if I had already dwelt for years within its walls. This lay grounded in my study, which at every step indeed pointed me to this metropolis of German art. One has not only not seen Germany if one does not know Munich; no, one above all does not know German art if one did not see Munich. At any rate this time before the war was the happiest and by far most contented of my life. If my earnings were still always very meagre, I yet did not live in order to be able to paint, but painted in order thereby only to secure for myself the possibility of my life, or better, to permit myself thereby my further study. I possessed the conviction of one day nevertheless attaining my goal which I had set myself. And this alone already let me bear all the other small cares of daily existence lightly and unconcernedly.

[kap4:2]
To this, however, there came still the inner love which seized me for this city more than for any other place known to me, almost already from the first hour of my stay. A German city!! What a difference against Vienna! I became sick when I only thought back to this race-Babylon. To this the dialect, lying much nearer to me, which especially in intercourse with Lower Bavarians could remind me of my sometime youth time. There were indeed a thousand and more things which were or became inwardly dear and precious to me.

[kap4:3]
Germany's False Alliance Policy

[kap4:4]
Most of all, however, there attracted me the wonderful marriage of primeval force and fine artistic mood, this unique line from the Hofbräuhaus to the Odeon, Oktoberfest to the Pinakothek, etc. That I today hang on this city more than on any other spot of earth on this world lies probably co-grounded in the fact that it is and remains inseparably bound up with the development of my own life; that, however, I already then received the happiness of a truly inner contentment was only to be ascribed to the magic which the wonderful Wittelsbach residence probably exercises upon every man blessed not only with a calculating understanding, but also with a feeling-full temper.

[kap4:5]
What besides my professional work attracted me most was also here again the study of the political day-events, among them especially foreign-political processes. I came to the latter over the detour of the German alliance policy, which I from my Austrian times on already held to be unconditionally false. To be sure, in Vienna the full extent of this self-deception of the Reich had not yet become quite clear to me. I was then inclined to assume — or perhaps also merely talked it into myself as an excuse — that one possibly in Berlin already knew how weak and little reliable the ally in reality would be, yet out of more or less mysterious grounds held back with this insight, in order to support an alliance policy which indeed Bismarck himself had once founded and whose sudden breaking-off could not be desirable, already in order not somehow to startle the lurking foreign country or to disquiet the inner philistine.

[kap4:6]
To be sure, the intercourse, above all in the people itself, let me to my horror already in a short time see that this belief was false. To my astonishment I had everywhere to establish that over the essence of the Habsburg monarchy even in the otherwise well-instructed circles there was present not a pale glimmer. Precisely in the people one was captured in the delusion of being allowed to regard the ally as an earnest power which in the hour of need would surely at once stand its man. In the mass one always held the monarchy for a "German" state and believed also to be able to build thereon. One was of the opinion that the force also here could be measured according to the millions, just as perhaps in Germany itself, and forgot

[kap4:7]
completely that first: Austria had already long ceased to be a German state system; but that second: the inner relations of this realm pressed from hour to hour more toward dissolution.

[kap4:8]
I had then known this state structure better than this so-called official "diplomacy," which blindly, as almost always, staggered toward doom; for the mood of the people was always only the outflow of that which one from above funnelled into the public opinion. From above, however, one drove with the "ally" a cult as around the golden calf. One hoped probably to replace through amiability what was lacking in uprightness. Thereby one always took words for bare values.

Already in Vienna anger seized me when I considered the difference which from time to time stepped into appearance between the speeches of the official statesmen and the content of the Vienna press. Thereby Vienna was indeed still, at least according to appearance, a German city. How otherwise, however, lay things when one came from Vienna, or better from German-Austria away, into the Slavic provinces of the realm! One needed only to take Prague newspapers into the hand in order to know how the whole sublime juggle-play of the Triple Alliance was judged there. There for this "statesmanly master-work" there was present nothing more than bloody scorn and mockery. In the deepest peace, when the two emperors precisely pressed the friendship kisses upon one another's forehead, one made no secret at all of it that this alliance would be settled on the day on which one would try to transfer it out of the shimmer of the Nibelung ideal into practical reality.

[kap4:10]
How one had indeed agitated oneself some years later, when in the finally come hour, in which the alliances should prove themselves, Italy sprang out of the Triple Alliance and let the two comrades go, indeed finally even itself became the enemy! That one at large even for one minute earlier dared to venture to believe in the possibility of such a wonder, namely the wonder that Italy would fight together with Austria, could be simply incomprehensible to everyone just not stricken with diplomatic blindness. Alone things lay indeed in Austria itself not a hair otherwise.

[kap4:11]
Bearers of the alliance thought were in Austria only the Habsburgs and the Germans. The Habsburgs out of calculation and compulsion, the Germans out of good faith and political — stupidity. Out of good faith, for they intended through the Triple Alliance to render to the German Reich itself a great

For in that the Habsburgs through the alliance with the Reich believed to be able to be sure against an interference from this side, and unfortunately also with right could be, they were able to carry through their inner policy of the slow displacement of the Germandom already essentially more easily and more risklessly. Not only that one with the well-known "objectivity" needed to fear no objection from the side of the Reich government at all, one could also at any time, with the pointing to the alliance, at once bring to silence the too-loud mouth of the Austrian Germandom itself, which wanted to open itself against a perhaps too base kind of Slavisation.

Should he offer resistance, in order then in the whole German public to be branded as a traitor to his own people-body? He, who for decades had brought the most unheard-of sacrifices precisely for his people-body?

Was not the value of the Triple Alliance for Germany downright dependent on the preservation of the German predominance in Austria? The adjustment of the official German diplomacy as well as also that of the whole public opinion to the inner-Austrian nationality problem was already no longer stupid, but simply insane! One built upon an alliance, adjusted the future and security of a seventy-million people thereon — and looked on as the only foundation for this federation was with the partner from year to year, plannedly and unswervingly surely, destroyed. One day there then had to remain over a "treaty" with the Vienna diplomacy; the federal help of a realm, however, would be lost.

What the House of Habsburg had sinned against Italian freedom and independence in the course of the centuries was too great for one to have been able to forget this, even if the will to it had been present. For Italy there were therefore also only two possibilities in the living-together with Austria: either alliance or war.

Especially since the relation of Austria to Russia pressed ever more toward a warlike confrontation, the German alliance policy was just as senseless as dangerous.

[kap4:20]
It was a classical case on which the lack of every great and right line of thinking could be pointed out.

[kap4:21]
Why did one then close an alliance at all? Indeed only in order so to be able to preserve the future of the Reich better than it, placed upon itself alone, would have been in the position to do. But this future of the Reich was indeed nothing else than the question of the preservation of the existence-possibility of the German people.

[kap4:22]
The Four Ways of German Politics

[kap4:23]
Consequently, however, the question could then only run: How must the life of the German nation shape itself in a tangible future, and how can one then guarantee to this development the needed foundations and the required security in the frame of the general European power relations?

[kap4:24]
At clear consideration of the presuppositions for the foreign-political activity of German statecraft one had to arrive at the following conviction: Germany has a yearly increase of population of nearly nine hundred thousand souls. The difficulty of the nourishment of this army of new state citizens must become greater from year to year and one day end at a catastrophe, in case precisely means and ways are not found still in time to forestall the danger of this famine-impoverishment.

[kap4:25]
There were four ways in order to escape such a terrible future development:

[kap4:26]
1. One could, after the French model, artificially restrict the increase of births and therewith meet an overpopulation.

[kap4:27]
Nature herself indeed, in times of great need or evil climatic relations as well as at poor soil yield, is wont also to stride to a restriction of the multiplication of the population of definite countries or races; to be sure in a just as wise as ruthless method. She does not hinder the capability of procreation in itself, but indeed the further preservation of the procreated, in that she exposes this to such heavy tests and privations that all less strong, less healthy is forced to return again into the bosom of the eternally Unknown. What she then nevertheless lets outlast the hardships of existence is thousandfold tested, hard, and well fit again to procreate further, so that the thorough selection may be able to begin again from the front. In that she so proceeds brutally against the individual and calls him at once again to herself as soon as he is not grown to the storm of life, she preserves the race and kind itself powerful, indeed heightens it to highest achievements.

[kap4:28]
Therewith, however, the diminution of the number is a strengthening of the person, hence however in the last end a fortification of the kind.

[kap4:29]
Otherwise is it when man sets himself to undertake a restriction of his number. He is not carved out of the wood of nature, but

[kap4:30]
"human." He understands it better than this cruel queen of all wisdom. He restricts not the further preservation of the individual, but rather the procreation itself. This appears to him, who indeed always only sees himself and never the race, to be more human and more justified than the reversed way. Alone unfortunately the consequences are also reversed:

[kap4:31]
While nature, in that she gives procreation free but subjects the further preservation to a heaviest test, out of a surplus number of individual beings chooses out the best as worth to life, receives them therefore alone and likewise lets them become bearers of the further preservation of their kind, man restricts the procreation, cares, however, cramp-like that every once-born being at every price also be preserved. This correction of the divine will seems to him just as wise as human, and he rejoices to have once again in a matter trumped nature, indeed proved her insufficiency. That in reality, to be sure, indeed the number was restricted, but for it also the worth of the individual was diminished, the dear little ape of the Allfather, to be sure, wants to see or hear only unwillingly.

[kap4:32]
For as soon as procreation as such is once restricted and the number of births diminished, there steps in place of the natural fight for existence, which lets only the all-strongest and healthiest at life, the self-evident addiction to "save" also the weakest, indeed most sickly, at every price, wherewith the germ is laid to a posterity which must become ever more miserable, the longer this mockery of nature and of her will holds on.

[kap4:33]
The end, however, will be that from such a people one day the existence on this world will be taken; for man can indeed for a certain time defy the eternal laws of the will of further preservation, alone the revenge comes sooner or later yet. A stronger generation will chase away the weak, since the urge to life in its last form will ever again break all the ridiculous fetters of a so-called humanity of the individuals, in order in its place to let step the humanity of nature, which annihilates weakness in order to present the place to strength.

[kap4:34]
Whoever therefore wants to secure to the German people existence on the way of a self-restriction of its multiplication robs it therewith of the future.

[kap4:35]
2. A second way would be that which we also today again hear proposed and praised often and often: the inner colonization. It is a proposal which is meant as well by just as many as it by most, however,

[kap4:36]
is wont to be badly understood, in order to cause the greatest thinkable damage which one can only imagine to oneself.

[kap4:37]
Without doubt the yield-capability of a soil can be heightened up to a definite border. Alone precisely only up to a definite border and not endlessly further. A certain time one will therefore, without famine-danger, be able to balance out the multiplication of the German people through a utilization-heightening of our soil. Alone to this stands opposite the fact that the demands upon life in general rise faster than even the number of the population. The demands of men in respect of nourishment and clothing become greater from year to year and stand already now, for example, in no relation more to the needs of our ancestors perhaps a hundred years ago. It is therefore erroneous to mean that every heightening of production creates the presupposition for a multiplication of the population: No; this applies only up to a certain degree, in that at least a part of the surplus products of the soil is used up for the satisfaction of the heightened needs of men. Alone even at greatest restriction on the one side and most industrious diligence on the other, there will nevertheless also here one day come a border which is drawn by the soil then itself. It will at all diligence no longer succeed to wrest more out of it, and then, if also pushed out for a certain time, the doom steps again into appearance. The hunger will at first from time to time, when bad harvests, etc., come, set itself in again. It will do this with rising people-number ever more often, so that he finally only then no longer steps up when rare richest years fill the stores. But there finally approaches the time in which also then the need will no longer be to be satisfied and hunger has become the eternal companion of such a people. Now nature must again help and make selection among those chosen by her to life; or man helps himself again himself, that is, he seizes to the artificial hindrance of his multiplication with all its already hinted heavy consequences for race and kind.

[kap4:38]
One will still be able to object that this future indeed stands before the whole of humanity one way or another, hence also the individual people naturally is not able to escape this doom.

[kap4:39]
This is at the first glance without further ado right. Nevertheless, however, here the following is to be considered:

[kap4:40]
Surely at a definite point of time the whole of humanity will be forced, in consequence of the impossibility of adjusting the fruitfulness of the soil to the further-rising people-number any longer, to set the multiplication of the human race and either to let nature decide again or through self-help, if possible then, to be sure, already on the righter way than today, to create the needed balance. Alone this will then precisely meet all peoples, while at the time only those races are met by such need which no longer possess force and strength enough to secure to themselves the soil needed for them on this world. For things lie indeed so, that on this earth at the time there is still always soil present unused in quite monstrous surfaces and only awaits the cultivator. Just so, however, it is also right that this soil was not by nature in and for itself lifted up for a definite nation or race as reserve surface for the future, but he is land and soil for the people which possesses the force to take him and the diligence to cultivate him.

[kap4:41]
Nature knows no political borders. She sets the living beings first upon this globe and looks on at the free play of forces. The strongest in courage and diligence then receives, as her dearest child, the master-right of existence pronounced.

[kap4:42]
If a people restricts itself to inner colonization, while other races clamp themselves fast upon ever greater soil surfaces of this earth, it will be forced to seize to self-restriction already at a time at which the other peoples still lastingly procreate themselves forth. Once, however, this case enters, and indeed the earlier, the smaller the living-space standing at disposal of a people is. Since in general unfortunately only too often the best nations or, still more rightly, the only truthful culture-races, the bearers of all human progress, in their pacifist blinding resolve to renounce new soil-acquisition, in order to content themselves with "inner" colonization, inferior nations, however, understand to secure to themselves monstrous living surfaces on this world, this would lead to the following end-result:

[kap4:43]
The culturally better, alone less ruthless races would already at a time have to limit their multiplication in consequence of their restricted soil, while the culturally deeper, but nature-like more brutal peoples in consequence of greatest living surfaces would still be in the position to procreate themselves forth into the unlimited. In other words: The world will therewith one day come into the

[kap4:44]
possession of the culturally less worthy, however more deed-powerful, humanity.

[kap4:45]
Then there are in a, if also ever so distant, future only two possibilities: Either the world is ruled according to the conceptions of our modern democracy, then the heavy-weight of every decision falls out in favour of the in number stronger races; or the world is dominated according to the laws of the natural order of force, then the peoples of brutal will conquer, and hence precisely again not the nation of self-restriction.

[kap4:46]
That, however, this world will one day be exposed to heaviest fights for the existence of humanity can no one doubt. In the end there conquers eternally only the addiction of self-preservation. Under it melts the so-called humanity as expression of a mixture of stupidity, cowardice, and imagined better-knowledge, like snow in the March sun. In eternal fight humanity has become great — in eternal peace it perishes.

[kap4:47]
For us Germans, however, the parole of "inner colonization" is already therefore unsalutary, since it at once strengthens in us the opinion of having found a means which, corresponding to the pacifist disposition, permits in gentle slumber-life to be able to "work out" existence for oneself. This doctrine, once earnestly taken with us, means the end of every exertion to preserve on this world the place which also is due to us. As soon as first the average German received the conviction of being able also on such a way to secure to himself life and the future, every attempt of an active and therewith alone fruitful representation of German life-necessities would be settled. Every really useful foreign policy, however, could through such an adjustment of the nation be regarded as buried, and with it the future of the German people at large.

[kap4:48]
In knowledge of these consequences it is not accidentally in the first line always the Jew who tries and understands to plant such death-dangerous thought-courses into our people. He knows his Pappenheimer only too well not to know that they gratefully fall victim to every Spanish treasure-swindler who understands to make them believe that the means would be found to play a trick on nature, to make the hard, unrelenting fight for existence superfluous, in order in its place soon through work, sometimes also

[kap4:49]
already through mere doing-nothing, according as "how it hits," to rise up to master of the planet.

[kap4:50]
It cannot be stressed sharply enough that every German inner colonization has in the first line only to serve to remove social abuses, above all to withdraw the soil from general speculation, never, however, can it suffice to secure the future of the nation without new ground and soil.

[kap4:51]
If we act otherwise, we shall in a short time not only be arrived at the end of our soil, but also at the end of our force.

[kap4:52]
Finally the following must still be established:

[kap4:53]
The restriction, lying in inner colonization, to a definite small soil surface, as also the same final effect, effected through cramping of procreation, leads to an extraordinarily unfavourable military-political situation of the nation concerned.

[kap4:54]
In the greatness of the dwelling-seat of a people there lies alone already an essential factor for the determination of its outer security. The greater the room quantity which stands at a people's disposal is, the greater is also its natural protection; for still always military decisions against peoples on a smaller pressed-together soil surface could be attained in faster and therewith, however, also lighter and especially more effective and more complete manner than this conversely can be possible against territorially extensive states. In the greatness of the state territory there lies therewith always still a certain protection against frivolous attacks, since a success thereby is to be attained only after long, heavy fights, hence the risk of an overbold attack will appear too great, so far as not quite extraordinary grounds lie present. Therefore there lodges already in the greatness of the state in itself a ground for the lighter preservation of the freedom and independence of a people, while conversely the smallness of such a structure downright challenges to taking-possession.

[kap4:55]
In fact also the two first possibilities for the creation of a balance between the rising people-number and the equally-great remaining soil were rejected in the so-called national circles of the Reich. The grounds for this position-taking were, to be sure, other than those adduced above: Toward the restriction of births one behaved oneself in the first line rejecting out of a certain moral feeling out; the inner colonization one pointed back with indignation, since one in it scented an attack

[kap4:56]
against the large landed property and therein saw the beginning of a general fight against private property at large. At the form in which especially this latter salvation-doctrine was recommended, one could also without further ado with such an assumption be right.

[kap4:57]
In general the defence of the broad mass against was not very clever and also met in no manner the core of the problem.

[kap4:58]
So there remained only still two ways to secure to the rising people-number work and bread.

[kap4:59]
3. One could either acquire new soil, in order to shove off the surplus millions yearly, and so to preserve the nation also further on the foundation of a self-nourishment, or one went

[kap4:60]
4. over to it, through industry and trade to create for foreign need, in order from the proceeds to contest existence.

[kap4:61]
Therefore: either soil- or colonial- and trade-policy.

[kap4:62]
Both ways were from different directions taken into the eye, tested, recommended and fought, until finally the last was definitively gone. The healthier way of both would, to be sure, be the former.

[kap4:63]
Acquisition of New Soil

[kap4:64]
The acquisition of new ground and soil for the settlement of the overflowing people-number possesses infinitely many advantages, especially if one takes not the present, but the future into the eye.

[kap4:65]
Already the possibility of the preservation of a healthy peasant estate as fundament of the whole nation can never be estimated high enough. Many of our present sufferings are only the consequence of the unhealthy relation between land- and city-people. A firm stock of small and middle peasants was still at all times the best protection against social illnesses as we possess them today. This is, however, also the only solution which lets a nation find the daily bread in the inner circulation of an economy. Industry and trade step back from their unhealthy leading position and fit themselves into the general frame of a national need- and balance-economy. Both are therewith no longer the foundation of the nourishment of the nation, but an auxiliary means of the same. In that they only more have as task the balance between own production and need on all domains, they make the whole people-nourishment more or less independent of the foreign country, help therefore along to secure the freedom of the state and the independence of the nation, especially in heavy days. To be sure, such a soil policy cannot perhaps in Cameroon find its fulfilment, but today almost exclusively only more in Europe. One must place oneself coolly and soberly upon the standpoint that it surely cannot be the intention of heaven to give to the one people fifty times as much of ground and soil on this world as to the other. One may in this case not let oneself be brought off by political borders from the borders of eternal right. If this earth really has room for all to life, then let one give to us therefore the soil necessary to us to life. One will, to be sure, not gladly do that. Then, however, the right of self-preservation steps into its effect; and what is refused to goodness, the fist has just to take for itself. Had our ancestors once made their decisions dependent on the same pacifist nonsense as the present of today, then we would at large possess only a third of our present soil as own; a German people, however, might then scarcely more have cares to bear in Europe. No — to the natural resoluteness for the fight for one's own existence we thank the two eastern marches of the Reich and

[kap4:66]
therewith that inner strength of the greatness of our state and people territory which alone at large let us exist until today.

[kap4:67]
Also out of another ground would this solution have been the right one:

[kap4:68]
Many European states resemble today pyramids set upon the point. Their European ground surface is ridiculously small as against their remaining burden in colonies, foreign trade, etc. One may say: point in Europe, basis in the whole world; in difference to the American Union, which still possesses the basis in its own continent and only with the point touches the rest of the earth. Hence there comes, however, also the unheard-of inner force of this state and the weakness of most European colonial powers. Also England is no proof against it, since one only too easily in view of the British Empire forgets the Anglo-Saxon world as such. The position of England can, in consequence of its language and culture community with the American Union alone already, be compared with no other state in Europe.

[kap4:69]
For Germany there lay accordingly the only possibility for the carrying-through of a healthy soil policy only in the acquisition of new land in Europe itself. Colonies can serve this purpose so long not as they do not appear fit for settlement with Europeans in the greatest measure. On a peaceful way, however, such colonial territories were in the nineteenth century no longer to be attained. It would consequently also such a colonial policy have been to be carried through only on the way of a heavy fight, which, however, then more expediently would have been fought out not for extra-European territories, but rather for land in the home-continent itself.

[kap4:70]
Such a resolution, however, demands undivided devotion. It does not go on to step toward a task with half means or even only hesitatingly, whose carrying-through appears possible only under tension of, however, also the last energy. Then also the whole political leadership of the Reich had to pay homage to this exclusive purpose; never was a step allowed to take place, led by other considerations than by the knowledge of this task and its conditions. One had to procure clarity for oneself that this goal was to be reached only under fight, and had then, however, also to look the passage of arms calmly and composedly in the eye.

With England against Russia

[kap4:72]
So all the alliances were exclusively to be tested from this point of view and to be estimated according to their usability. If one wanted in Europe ground and soil, then this could in the large and whole only take place at the cost of Russia; then the new Reich had to set itself again in march on the street of the sometime knights of the Order, in order with the German sword to give to the German plow the clod, to the nation, however, the daily bread.

[kap4:73]
For such a policy there was, however, in Europe only a single ally: England.

[kap4:74]
Only with England alone was one able, the back covered, to begin the new German-march. The right to this would not have been less than the right of our ancestors. None of our pacifists refuses to eat the bread of the East, although the first plow once was called "sword"!

[kap4:75]
To win England's inclination, however, no sacrifice was allowed to be too great.

[kap4:76]
It was to renounce colonies and sea-validity, but to spare the British industry the competition.

[kap4:77]
Only unconditional clear adjustment alone could lead to such a goal. Renunciation of world trade and colonies, renunciation of a German war fleet, concentration of the entire means of might of the state upon the land army.

[kap4:78]
The result would probably have been a momentary restriction, alone a great and mighty future.

[kap4:79]
There was a time at which England in this sense would have let talk with itself. Since it had very well grasped that Germany in consequence of its increase of population must seek after some way out and either with England would find this in Europe or without England in the world.

With England, however, such a business could very well be made. British diplomacy was still always clever enough to know that without counter-achievement no achievement is to be expected.

Let one, however, imagine that a clever German foreign policy had taken over the role of Japan in the year 1904, and one can scarcely measure what consequences this would have had for Germany.

It would never have come to a "World War."

For this state mummy bound itself with Germany not for the fighting-through of a war, but for the preservation of an eternal peace, which then could be used in clever manner for the slow but sure extermination of the Germandom of the monarchy.

Solution of the Austrian Alliance

[kap4:86]

If Germany did not possess so much national reflection and also ruthlessness as to tear away from the impossible Habsburg state the disposal over the fate of the ten million tribal-comrades, then one truly was not allowed to expect that it would ever offer the hand to such far-looking and daring plans. The attitude of the old Reich to the Austrian question was the touchstone for its conduct in the fate-fight of the whole nation.

[kap4:87]
On all cases one was not allowed to look on as year by year the Germandom was pushed back more, since indeed the worth of Austria's alliance-capability was exclusively determined by the preservation of the German element. Alone, one did not tread this way at all.
