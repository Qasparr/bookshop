As a young scamp in my exuberant years nothing had so much grieved me as precisely to be born in a time which evidently would erect its temples of fame only more to shopkeepers or state officials. The waves of the historical events seemed already to have laid themselves so that really only to the "peaceful competition of the peoples," that is therefore to a quiet mutual cheating under switching-off of violent methods of defence, the future seemed to belong. The individual states began ever more to resemble undertakings which mutually dig away the soil, catch away the customers and orders, and try to over-advantage one another in every manner, and set all this in scene under a just as great as harmless screaming. But this development seemed not only to hold on, but should one day (after general recommendation) remodel the whole world into a single great department store, in whose forehalls then the busts of the most cunning shovers and most harmless administration officials would be stored up for immortality. The merchants could then be furnished by the English, the administration officials by the Germans; as owners, however, the Jews would probably have to sacrifice themselves up, since they after their own confession indeed never earn anything, but eternally only "pay," and besides speak most languages.

Why could one then not have been born a hundred years earlier? Say at the time of the Wars of Liberation, when the man really, also without "business," was still worth something?!

[kap5:2]
I had so often made myself annoyed thoughts over my earthly wandering, begun as it seemed to me too late, and regarded the time of "rest and order" standing before me as an unmerited baseness of fate. I was indeed already as a boy no "pacifist," and all educational attempts in this direction became blanks.

[kap5:3]

[kap5:4]

I saw in a defeat of the Russians also a defeat of Austrian Slavdom.

The Approaching Catastrophe

[kap5:6]
Since then many years had passed, and what once as a boy appeared to me like rotten lingering sickness I now felt as rest before the storm. Already during my Vienna time there lay over the Balkans that pale sultriness which is wont to indicate the hurricane, and already sometimes there twitched up also a brighter light-shine, in order, however, quickly to lose itself again into the uncanny darkness. Then, however, came the Balkan War, and with it the first gust of wind swept over the nervously become Europe. The now coming time lay like a heavy nightmare upon men, brooding like feverish tropic heat, so that the feeling of the approaching catastrophe in consequence of the eternal care finally became longing: heaven might finally grant to fate, which was no longer to be checked, the free course. Then there also already drove the first mighty lightning-stroke down upon the earth: the weather broke loose, and into the thunder of heaven there mixed itself the droning of the batteries of the World War. When the news of the murder of the Archduke Franz Ferdinand arrived in Munich (I sat precisely at home and heard only inexactly the course of the deed), there seized me at first care that the bullets might perhaps stem from the pistols of German students who, out of indignation over the lasting Slavisation work of the successor to the throne, wanted to free the German people from this inner enemy. What the consequence of that would have been, one could at once think out to oneself: a new wave of persecutions, which now before the whole world would have been "justified" and "grounded." When, however, right thereupon I already heard the names of the presumed doers and besides read their establishment as Serbs, soft horror began to creep over me over this revenge of inscrutable fate.

The Greatest Friend of the Slavs Murdered

[kap5:8]
The greatest friend of the Slavs fell under the bullets of Slavic fanatics.

[kap5:9]
Whoever in the last years possessed opportunity durably to observe the relation of Austria to Serbia could indeed scarcely for a moment be in doubt over it that the stone had come into rolling, at which a holding-up could no longer be given.

[kap5:10]

One had ground for the fear that this case had to come at the latest with the death of the old Emperor; then, however, the monarchy was perhaps at large no longer in the position to offer earnest resistance. The whole state stood in the last years already so much on the two eyes of Franz Joseph that the death of this age-old embodiment of the Reich in the feeling of the broad mass from the outset counted as the death of the Reich itself. Yes, it belonged with to the cleverest arts of especially Slavic politics to awaken the appearance that the Austrian state anyway owed its existence only more to the quite wonderful, unique art of this monarch; a flattery which in the Hofburg did so much the better as it corresponded least to the real merits of this Emperor. The sting which lurked hidden in this praise one was not able to find out. One did not see, or wanted perhaps also there no longer to see, that, the more the monarchy was only more adjusted to the towering government art, as one was wont to express oneself, of this "wisest monarch" of all times, the more catastrophic the situation had to become when one day also here fate knocked at the door in order to fetch its tribute.

[kap5:11]
Was the old Austria without the old Emperor then at large still thinkable?! Would not at once the tragedy which once had met Maria Theresa have repeated itself?

The Austrian Ultimatum

[kap5:13]
No, one really does the Vienna government circles injustice when the reproach is made to them that they now drove to the war, which otherwise perhaps yet could have been avoided. Alone this was indeed the curse of the German as well as of the Austrian diplomacy, that it indeed always had already tried to push out the unavoidable reckoning, until it finally was forced to strike at the most unhappy hour. One can be convinced that a once-more attempt to save the peace would have brought the war at a still more unfavourable time all the more.

[kap5:14]
No, whoever did not want this war had also to bring up the courage to draw the consequences. These, however, would have consisted only in the sacrificing of Austria. Thereby one then had to resolve to join in or just to look on, in order with empty hands to let fate have its course.

[kap5:15]
Precisely those, however, who today curse most of all over the beginning of the war and judge most wisely were those who most doomfully helped along to steer into it.

Now one had to bear the consequences of this madness. What came had to come and was under no circumstances more to be avoided. The guilt of the German government was thereby that it, in order to preserve the peace only indeed, always missed the favourable hours of striking, entangled itself into the alliance for the preservation of world peace, and so finally became the victim of a world coalition which precisely opposed the resoluteness to the World War to the urge toward preservation of world peace.

[kap5:17]
Had, however, the Vienna government then given to the ultimatum another, milder form, this would at the matter have changed nothing more at all than

[kap5:18]

The fight of the year 1914 was to the masses, truthful God, not forced on, but desired by the whole people itself.

[kap5:20]
One wanted finally to prepare an end to a general insecurity. Only so can one also understand that to this heaviest wrestling over two million German men and boys voluntarily placed themselves to the flag, ready to shield it with the last drop of blood.

The German Fight for Freedom

[kap5:22]
To me myself the hours of that time came before like a redemption out of the annoyed feelings of youth. I am also today not ashamed to say it, that I, overwhelmed by stormy enthusiasm, had sunk into the knees and thanked heaven out of overfull heart that it had presented me the happiness to be allowed to live in this time.

[kap5:23]
A fight for freedom had broken out such as the earth had seen no mightier one hitherto; for as soon as doom had only begun its course, there also already dawned upon the broadest masses the conviction that it was this time not a matter of Serbia's or also Austria's fate, but of being or not-being of the German nation.

[kap5:24]
For the last time on many years the people had become clairvoyant over its own future. So there came then also right at the beginning of the monstrous wrestling into the intoxication of an exuberant enthusiasm the needed earnest undertone: for this knowledge alone let the national elevation become more than a mere straw fire. But the earnestness was only too much required; one made oneself then in general indeed not the least conception of the possible length and duration of the now beginning fight. One dreamed of being home again in winter, in order then to continue in renewed peaceful work.

[kap5:25]
What man wants, that he hopes and believes. The overwhelming majority of the nation was of the eternal unsure condition already long weary to disgust; so it was also only understandable that one no longer believed at all in a peaceful settling of the Austrian-Serbian conflict, but hoped for the final confrontation. To these millions belonged also I.

[kap5:26]
Scarcely was the tidings of the assassination become known in Munich, so there twitched through my head at once also two thoughts: first, that the war would finally be unavoidable; further, however, that now the Habsburg state was forced also to hold the federation; for what I always had feared most was the possibility that Germany itself one day, perhaps precisely in consequence of this alliance, could fall into a conflict, without, however, Austria having given the direct occasion hereto, and so the Austrian state out of inner-political grounds would not bring up the force of the resolution to place itself behind the confederate. The Slavic majority of the Reich would such a self-made intention at once

[kap5:27]
have begun to sabotage and would always still rather have smashed the whole state into rubble than granted to the confederate the demanded help. This danger, however, was now removed. The old state had to fight, one might want it or not.

The Sense of the Fight for Freedom

[kap5:29]
My own position to the conflict was to me likewise very simple and clear; for me there strove not Austria for some Serbian satisfaction, but Germany for its standing, the German nation for being or not-being, for freedom and future. Bismarck's work now had to beat itself; what the fathers once in the battles from Weißenburg to Sedan and Paris had fought out with their hero-blood had now young Germany to earn itself anew. If this fight, however, was victoriously stood, then our people was in the circle of the great nations also again entered at outer might; then first again could the German Reich prove itself as a mighty ward of peace, without having to shorten its children the daily bread for the sake of dear peace.

[kap5:30]
I had once as a boy and young man so often had the wish to be able at least once also through deeds to testify that national enthusiasm is to me no empty delusion. It often came before me almost as a sin to scream Hurra without perhaps also only possessing the inner right hereto; for who was allowed to use this word without having once tested it there where all play-acting is at an end and the unrelenting hand of the goddess of fate begins to weigh peoples and men upon truth and standing of their disposition? So there welled, as with millions of others, indeed also my heart over with proud happiness at being able now finally to redeem myself from this paralyzing feeling. I had so often sung "Deutschland über alles" and out of a full throat called Heil, that it appeared to me almost like a subsequently granted grace now in the divine service of the eternal Judge to be allowed to step forth as witness for the attestation of the truthfulness of this disposition. For it stood firm with me from the first hour that I in the case of a war — which seemed to me unavoidable — so or so would at once leave the books. Just so, however, I also knew that my place then had to be there where the inner voice now once pointed me.

[kap5:31]
Out of political grounds I had left Austria in the first line; but what was more self-evident than that I now, since the fight began, had to take account of this disposition all the more! I did not want to fight for the Habsburg state, but was ready for my people and the Reich embodying this to die at any time.

Entry into a Bavarian Regiment

[kap5:33]
On 3 August I handed in an immediate-petition to His Majesty King Ludwig III with the request to be allowed to enter into a Bavarian regiment. The cabinet chancellery in these days surely had not little to do; so much the greater was my joy when I already on the day thereupon received the settlement of my request. When I with trembling hands had opened the writing and read the approval of my request with the summons to report myself at a Bavarian regiment, jubilation and thankfulness knew no borders. Few days later I then wore the tunic which I was only after nearly six years to take off again.

[kap5:34]
So, as probably for every German, there began now also for me the most unforgettable and greatest time of my earthly life. Over against the events of this mightiest wrestling all that was past fell back into a stale nothing. With proud melancholy I think precisely in these days, when the mighty happening celebrates its tenth yearly return, back to those weeks of the beginning hero-fight of our people, which fate graciously permitted me to join in making.

[kap5:35]
As only yesterday picture upon picture draws past me; I see myself in the circle of my dear comrades dressed in, then for the first time march out, drill, etc., until finally the day of the march-out came.

[kap5:36]
A single care tormented me in this time, me as also so many others, whether we would not come too late to the front. This alone let me often and often find no rest. So there remained in every victory-jubilation over a new hero-deed hidden a soft drop of bitterness, since indeed with every new victory the danger of our coming-too-late seemed to rise.

The Baptism of Fire

[kap5:38]
And so there finally came the day on which we left Munich in order to step forth for the fulfilment of our duty. For the first time I so saw the Rhine, when we along its still waves drove toward the West, in order to shield it, the German stream of streams, before the greed of the old enemy.

[kap5:39]
When through the tender veil of the early fog the mild rays of the first sun let the Niederwald monument shimmer down upon us, there surged out of the endlessly long transport train the old Watch on the Rhine out into the morning heaven, and to me the breast wanted to become too narrow.

[kap5:40]
And then comes a moist, cold night in Flanders, through which we march silently, and when the day then begins to loose itself out of the fogs, there hisses suddenly an iron greeting over our heads toward us and strikes in sharp bang the little balls between our rows, whipping up the wet soil; before, however, the little cloud has even drawn away, there drones out of two hundred throats toward the first messenger of death the first Hurra. Then, however, it began to crackle and to drone, to sing and to howl, and with feverish eyes it now drew everyone forward, ever faster, until suddenly over beet fields and hedges the fight set in, the fight man against man. From the distance, however, the sounds of a song penetrated to our ear and came ever nearer and nearer, sprang over from company to company, and there, when death precisely busily reached into our rows, there the song also reached us, and we now gave it again further: Deutschland, Deutschland über alles, über alles in der Welt!

[kap5:41]
After four days we returned. Even the step had now become different. Seventeen-year boys now looked similar to men.

[kap5:42]
The volunteers of the List Regiment had perhaps not learned rightly to fight; alone, to die they knew like old soldiers.

[kap5:43]
That was the beginning.

From War-Volunteer to Old Soldier

[kap5:45]
So it now went further year for year; in place of battle-romance, however, horror had stepped. The enthusiasm cooled gradually off, and the exuberant jubilation was suffocated by the fear of death. There came the time in which everyone had to wrestle between the urge of self-preservation and the admonition of duty. Also to me this fight was not spared. Always, when death was on the hunt, an indefinite Something tried to revolt, was at pains to present itself as reason to the weak body, and was, however, only the cowardice which under such disguises tried to ensnare the individual. A heavy dragging and warning then began, and only the last rest of conscience often still gave the casting vote. The more, however, this voice, which admonished to caution, toiled itself, the louder and more penetratingly it enticed, the sharper then became the resistance, until finally after long inner strife the consciousness of duty carried off the victory. Already in the winter of 1915/16 this fight was with me decided. The will had finally become restlessly master. Could I storm along the first days with jubilation and laughing, so I was now quiet and resolved. This, however, was the lasting. Now first fate could stride to the last tests, without the nerves tearing or the understanding failing.

[kap5:46]
Out of the young war-volunteer an old soldier had become.

[kap5:47]

[kap5:48]

Now, after two, three years, during which it was thrown out of one battle into the other, always fighting against superior might in number and weapons, suffering hunger and enduring privations, now was the time to test the goodness of this unique army.

A Memorial of Immortality

[kap5:50]
May millennia pass, so one will never be allowed to speak and say of heroism without thinking of the German army of the World War. Then out of the veil of the past out the iron front of the grey steel helmet will become visible, not wavering and not yielding, a memorial of immortality. So long, however, as Germans live, they will consider that these once were sons of their people.

[kap5:51]
I was then a soldier and did not want to politicize. It was for this really also not the time. I cherish today still the conviction that the last drayman has performed for the fatherland still always more of valuable services than even the first, say, "parliamentarian." I never hated these chatterers more than precisely in the time in which every truthful fellow who had something to say screamed this into the face of the enemy or else expediently left his mouth-work at home and silently somewhere did his duty. Yes, I hated then all these "politicians," and had it come upon me, there would at once have been formed a parliamentary digger-battalion; then they could have chattered among themselves to heart's desire and need, without annoying or even damaging decent and honest humanity.

[kap5:52]
I wanted therefore then to know nothing of politics, could, however, not do otherwise than take a position toward certain appearances which now once concerned the whole nation, but especially applied to us soldiers.

Artificial Damping of the Enthusiasm

[kap5:54]
Two things it was which then inwardly annoyed me and which I held harmful.

[kap5:55]
Already after the first victory-news a certain press began slowly, and perhaps for many at first unrecognizably, to let some drops of wormwood fall into the general enthusiasm. One feared that this in this form was not worthy of so great a nation and therewith also not corresponding. The bravery and the heroism of the German soldier would indeed be something quite self-evident, so that one over it should not let oneself be carried away so much by unconsidered outbursts of joy, already for the sake of the foreign country, to which a still and worthy form of joy gives more promise than an unbridled shouting, etc. Finally we Germans should yet also now not forget that the war was not our intention; hence we also should not have to be ashamed to confess openly and manfully that we at any time would contribute our part to a reconciliation of humanity. Therefore, however, it would not be clever to blacken the purity of the deeds of the army through too great screaming, since indeed the rest of the world for such a behaviour would bring up only little understanding. Nothing does one admire more than the modesty with which a true hero — forgets his deeds silently and quietly, for thereupon the whole came out.

[kap5:56]
Instead of now taking such a fellow by his long ears and drawing him up to a long pole and on a rope, in order that the ink-knight no longer be able to insult the celebrating nation in its aesthetic feeling, one actually began to proceed against the "unfitting" kind of victory-jubilation with admonitions.

[kap5:57]

But how should one without this might of enthusiasm stand a fight which after human discretion would place the most monstrous demands upon the soul-properties of the nation?

[kap5:58]
I knew the psyche of the broad mass only too exactly not to know that one here with "aesthetic" elevation would not be able to fan the fire which was necessary in order to hold this iron in warmth. One was in my eyes mad that one did nothing to increase the boiling heat of passion; that one, however, also still cut down the happily present one, I was utterly unable to understand.

The Application of Naked Force

[kap5:73]
Freely then, however, a question came to validity: Can one then exterminate spiritual ideas at large with the sword? Can one with the application of raw force fight "world-views"?

[kap5:74]
I have laid this question before myself already at that time oftener than once.

[kap5:75]
At the thinking-through of analogous cases, which let themselves be found especially on religious ground in history, there results the following fundamental knowledge:

[kap5:76]
Conceptions and ideas as well as movements with a determinate spiritual ground, be this now false or true, can from a certain point of time of their becoming on be broken with means of might of a technical kind only more then when these bodily weapons at the same time themselves are bearers of a new kindling thought, an idea or world-view.

[kap5:77]
The application of force alone, without the driving force of a spiritual ground-conception as presupposition, can never lead to the annihilation of an idea and its spreading, except in the form of a restless extermination of even the last bearer and the destruction of the last tradition. This, however, mostly means the exit of such a state body out of the circle of might-political significance for often endless time, sometimes also for ever; for such a blood-sacrifice meets indeed, as experience shows, the best part of folkdom, since indeed every persecution which takes place without spiritual presupposition appears as morally not justified and now whips up precisely the more valuable stocks of a people to protest, which, however, expresses itself in an appropriation of the spiritual content of the unjustly persecuted movement. This happens with many then simply out of the feeling of opposition against the attempt of the clubbing-down of an idea through brutal force. Thereby, however, the number of the inner adherents grows in just the measure in which the persecution increases. Hence the restless annihilation of the new doctrine will only be to be carried out on the way of an extermination so great and ever increasing itself that thereover finally to the people or also state concerned all truly valuable blood at large is withdrawn. This, however, avenges itself, in that now indeed a so-called "inner" cleansing can take place, alone at the cost of a general impotence. Always, however, such a process will from the outset already be in vain if the doctrine to be fought has already overstepped a certain small circle.

[kap5:78]
Therefore also here, as with all growth, the first time of childhood is still most readily exposed to the possibility of an annihilation, while with rising years the force of resistance increases, in order first at approaching weakness of age to yield again to new youth, if also in another form and out of other grounds.

[kap5:79]
Actually, however, almost all attempts to exterminate through force without spiritual ground a doctrine and its organizational effect lead to failures, indeed end not seldom precisely with the opposite of the wished-for, out of the following ground:

[kap5:80]
The very first presupposition to a manner of fighting with the weapons of naked force is and remains persistence. That means that only in the durably even application of the methods for the suppression of a doctrine, etc., lies the possibility of the succeeding of the intention. As soon as here, however, even only waveringly force alternates with indulgence, not only will the doctrine to be suppressed ever again recover itself, but it will even be in the position to draw new values out of every persecution, in that after the ebbing of such a wave of pressure the indignation over the suffered sorrow will lead new adherents to the old doctrine, the already present ones, however, will hang on it with greater defiance and deeper hate than before; indeed, already split-off renegades will after removal of the danger try to return to the old attitude. In the eternally even application of force alone lies the very first presupposition to success. This persistence, however, is always only the result of a determinate spiritual conviction. Every force which does not spring out of a firm spiritual ground will be wavering and unsure. It lacks the stability which is able to rest only in a fanatical world-view. It is the outflow of the respective energy and brutal resoluteness of an individual, hence however precisely subjected to the change of personality and its kind of essence and strength.

[kap5:81]
There comes, however, hereto still something else:

[kap5:82]
Every world-view, be it more of a religious or political kind — sometimes the border is here only hard to establish — fights less for the negative annihilation of the opposing world of ideas than rather for the positive carrying-through of its own. Therewith, however, its fight is less defence than attack. It is thereby already in the determination of the goal in advantage, since indeed this goal represents the victory of its own idea, while conversely it only

[kap5:83]
hard is to be determined when the negative goal of the annihilation of a hostile doctrine may be regarded as reached and secured. Already therefore the attack of the world-view will be more planned, but also mightier than the defence of such a one; as indeed at large also here the decision falls to the attack and not to the defence. The fight against a spiritual might with means of force is, however, so long only defence as the sword does not itself step forth as bearer, announcer, and spreader of a new spiritual doctrine.

[kap5:84]
The Attack of a World-View

[kap5:85]
One can therefore summarize and hold fast the following:

[kap5:86]
Every attempt to fight a world-view with means of might fails at the end so long as the fight does not receive the form of the attack for a new spiritual attitude. Only in the wrestling of two world-views with one another is the weapon of brutal force, persistently and ruthlessly set in, able to bring about the decision for the side supported by it.

[kap5:87]
At that, however, hitherto the fighting of Marxism had always still failed. That was the ground why also Bismarck's socialist legislation finally, despite everything, failed and had to fail. There lacked the platform of a new world-view for whose ascent the fight could have been fought. For that the babble of a so-called "state authority" or of "rest and order" could be a suitable ground for the spiritual drive of a fight on life and death, only the proverbial wisdom of higher ministerial officials will manage to opine.

[kap5:88]
But because a really spiritual bearer of this fight lacked, Bismarck also had to commit the carrying-out of his socialist legislation to the discretion and will of that institution which itself already was a birth of Marxist manner of thought. In that the iron Chancellor entrusted the fate of his Marxist war to the benevolence of bourgeois democracy, he made the buck into the gardener.

[kap5:89]
All this, however, was only the forced consequence of the lack of a fundamental new world-view, opposed to Marxism, of stormy will-to-conquest.

[kap5:90]
So the result of the Bismarckian fight was only a heavy disappointment.

Bourgeois Class Parties

[kap5:96]
One should at large beware of holding the broad mass for more stupid than it is. In political affairs the feeling not seldom decides more rightly than the understanding. The opinion, however, that for the incorrectness of this feeling of the mass its stupid international attitude would sufficiently speak, can at once in the most thorough manner be refuted through the simple indication that the pacifist democracy is not less mad; its bearers, however, stem almost exclusively out of the bourgeois camp. So long as still millions of citizens every morning devoutly worship their Jewish democratic press, it becomes these gentlefolk very badly to joke over the stupidity of the "comrade" who in the end only devours the same muck, if also just in another making-up. In both cases the manufacturer is one and the same Jew.

[kap5:97]
One should therefore very well beware of denying things which now once are.

[kap5:98]
The fact that in the class question it is by no means a matter only of ideal problems, as one especially before elections always likes to make believe, cannot be denied away. The caste arrogance of a great part of our people is, just as above all the lesser estimation of the hand-worker, an appearance which does not stem out of the fantasy of a moon-sick one.

[kap5:99]
It shows, however, quite apart from that, the slight thinking capacity of our so-called intelligence, when precisely in these circles it is not comprehended that a condition which was not able to prevent the coming-up of a pest as Marxism now once is will now however all the less be in the position to win back again the lost.

No Replacement for the Social Democracy

[kap5:101]
The "bourgeois" parties, as they designate themselves, will never more be able to fetter the "proletarian" masses to their camp, since here two worlds stand opposite one another, partly naturally, partly artificially separated, whose behaviour-condition toward one another can only be the fight. Conquer, however, here will the younger — and this would be Marxism.

[kap5:102]
Actually a fight against the Social Democracy in the year 1914 was indeed thinkable; alone, how long this condition at the lack of every practical replacement would have been to be upheld could be doubtful.

[kap5:103]
Here a great gap was present.
