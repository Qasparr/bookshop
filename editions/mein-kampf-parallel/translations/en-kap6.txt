[kap6:0]
At my attentive following of all political processes the activity of propaganda had always extraordinarily interested me. I saw in it an instrument which precisely the socialist-Marxist organizations mastered with masterly skill and understood to bring to application. I learned thereby already early to understand that the right use of propaganda represents a real art which to the bourgeois parties was and remained almost as good as unknown. Only the Christian-Social movement, especially in Lueger's time, brought it also on this instrument to a certain virtuosity and owed to it also very many of its successes.

[kap6:1]
To what monstrous results, however, a rightly applied propaganda is able to lead could first be seen during the war. Unfortunately, however, here again everything was to be studied on the other side, for the activity on our side remained indeed in this relation more than modest. Alone, precisely the so complete failure of the entire enlightenment on the German side, which especially had to spring grell into the eyes of every soldier, became with me the occasion now to occupy myself still much more penetratingly with the propaganda question.

[kap6:2]
Time for thinking was thereby often more than enough present; the practical instruction, however, the enemy imparted to us, unfortunately only too well.

[kap6:3]
For what with us here was missed, the opponent made up with unheard-of skill and truly genial reckoning. At this hostile war propaganda I also have infinitely learned. At the heads of those, however, who most of all would have had to let this be a lesson to themselves, the time passed by without trace; one fancied oneself there in part too clever to accept instructions from the others; in the other part, however, the honest will hereto lacked.

[kap6:4]
Propaganda a Means

[kap6:5]
Was there with us at large a propaganda?

[kap6:6]
Unfortunately I can answer thereupon only with No. Everything which in this direction was really undertaken was so insufficient and false from the beginning that it at the least was of no use, sometimes, however, directly caused damage.

[kap6:7]
In form insufficient, in essence psychologically false: this had to be the result of an attentive examination of German war propaganda. Already over the first question one seems not to have become quite clear, namely: Is propaganda means or purpose?

[kap6:8]
It is a means and must accordingly be judged from the point of view of the purpose. Its form will hence have to be one adapted expediently to the support of the goal which it serves. It is also clear that the significance of the goal can be a different one from the standpoint of the general need, and that therewith also propaganda in its inner value is differently determined. But the goal for which in the course of the war was fought was the most sublime and mightiest that can be thought for men: it was the freedom and independence of our people, the security of nourishment for the future, and — the honour of the nation; something which, despite the contrary opinion of today, nevertheless is present or better should be, since indeed peoples without honour are wont sooner or later to lose freedom and independence — which again only corresponds to a higher justice, since honourless generations of rags deserve no freedom. Whoever, however, wants to be a cowardly slave may and can have no honour at all, since indeed this otherwise would fall home to general contempt in the shortest time.

[kap6:9]
In the strife for a human existence the German people fought, and to support this strife would have been the purpose of the war's propaganda; to help him to victory had to be the goal.

[kap6:10]
The Purpose of Propaganda

[kap6:11]
When, however, peoples fight for their existence on this planet, hence the fate-question of being or not-being steps up to them, all considerations of humanity or aesthetics fall together into a nothing; for all these conceptions float not in the world-ether, but stem out of the fantasy of man and are bound to him. His departure from this world looses also these concepts again into nothing, for nature knows them not. They are, however, also among men only the own of few peoples, or better races, and indeed in that measure in which they themselves stem out of the feeling of the same. Humanity and aesthetics would even pass away in a humanly inhabited world as soon as this lost the races who are creators and bearers of these concepts.

[kap6:12]
Therewith, however, all these concepts at the fight of a people for its existence on this world have only subordinate significance, indeed separate out completely as determining for the forms of the fight as soon as through them the self-preservation force of a people lying in fight could be paralyzed. But that is always the only visible result.

[kap6:13]
As concerns the question of humanity, Moltke has already expressed himself to the effect that this in war always lies in the shortness of the procedure, therefore that to it the sharpest manner of fighting most corresponds.

[kap6:14]
When, however, one tries in such things to advance with the babble of aesthetics, etc., then there can thereupon really be only one answer: fate-questions of the significance of the existence-fight of a people lift every obligation to beauty. The un-beautiful-est thing which there can be in human life is and remains the yoke of slavery. Or does this Schwabing decadence perhaps feel the present lot of the German nation as "aesthetic"? With the Jews, as the modern inventors of this culture-perfume, one indeed truly does not need to entertain oneself over it. Their whole existence is the flesh-become protest against the aesthetics of the image of the Lord.

[kap6:15]
When, however, these points of view of humanity and beauty for the fight first once separate out, then they also cannot find use as a measure-staff for propaganda.

[kap6:16]
Propaganda Only for the Mass

[kap6:17]
Propaganda was in the war a means to the purpose; this, however, was the fight for the existence of the German people, and thus propaganda could also only be considered from the principles valid herefor. The cruelest weapons were then human when they conditioned the faster victory, and beautiful were only those methods alone which helped to secure to the nation the dignity of freedom.

[kap6:18]
This was the only possible position in such a fight on life and death to the question of war propaganda.

[kap6:19]
Had one become clear over it at the so-called authoritative places, so one would never have been able to come into that unsureness over the form and application of this weapon; for also this is only a weapon, if also a truly frightful one in the hand of the knower.

[kap6:20]
The second question of altogether decisive significance was the following: To whom has propaganda to address itself? To the scientific intelligence or to the less educated mass?

[kap6:21]
It has eternally to address itself only to the mass!

[kap6:22]
For the intelligence, or what today unfortunately frequently so names itself, propaganda is not there, but scientific instruction. Propaganda, however, is as little science in its content as, say, a placard is art in its presentation in itself. The art of the placard lies in the capability of the designer through form and colour to make the crowd attentive. The art-exhibition placard has only to point to the art of the exhibition; the more this succeeds to it, the greater is then the art of the placard itself. The placard shall further convey to the mass a conception of the significance of the exhibition, by no means, however, be a replacement of the art offered in this. Whoever therefore wants to occupy himself with the art itself must already study more than the placard; indeed, for him also mere "wandering through" the exhibition by no means suffices. Of him it may be expected that he in thorough looking lose himself into the individual works and then slowly form himself a just judgment.

[kap6:23]
The Task of Propaganda

[kap6:24]
Similarly lie the relations also with that which we today designate with the word propaganda.

[kap6:25]
The task of propaganda lies not in a scientific training of the individual, but in a pointing of the mass to determinate facts, processes, necessities, etc., whose significance thereby first shall be moved into the circle of sight of the mass.

[kap6:26]
The art now lies exclusively therein, to do this in so excellent a manner that a general conviction of the reality of a fact, the necessity of a process, the correctness of something necessary, etc., arises. Since it, however, is not necessity in itself and cannot be, since its task indeed, exactly as with the placard, has to consist in the making-attentive of the crowd and not in the instructing of the scientifically anyway experienced or of those striving after education and insight, so its working must also ever more be calibrated on the feeling and only very conditionally on the so-called understanding.

[kap6:27]
Every propaganda has to be folk-like and to set its spiritual level according to the receptivity of the most limited among those to whom it thinks to address itself. Therewith its purely spiritual height will be to be set so much the deeper, the greater the mass of men to be seized shall be. If, however, it is a matter, as with the propaganda for the holding-out of a war, of drawing a whole people into its sphere of effect, so the caution at the avoidance of too high spiritual presuppositions can never be great enough.

[kap6:28]
The more modest then its scientific ballast is, and the more it exclusively takes consideration on the feeling of the mass, so much the more striking the success. This, however, is the best proof for the correctness or incorrectness of a propaganda and not the succeeded satisfaction of some scholars or aesthetic youths.

[kap6:29]
Precisely therein lies the art of propaganda, that it, comprehending the feeling-world of conceptions of the great mass, in psychologically right form finds the way to the attentiveness and further to the heart of the broad mass. That this is not comprehended by our know-it-alls proves only their laziness of thought or conceit.

[kap6:30]
The Psychology of Propaganda

[kap6:31]
If one, however, comprehends the necessity of the setting of the advertising art of propaganda on the broad mass, there results further already therefrom the following doctrine:

[kap6:32]
It is false to want to give to propaganda the many-sidedness of, say, scientific instruction.

[kap6:33]
The receptivity of the great mass is only very limited, the understanding small; for it, however, the forgetfulness great. Out of these facts out, every effective propaganda has to limit itself to only very few points and to use these slogan-like so long until also determinately the last under such a word is able to conceive to himself the wished-for. As soon as one sacrifices this fundamental principle and wants to become many-sided, one will bring the effect to fluttering-apart, since the crowd is able neither to digest nor to retain the offered material. Therewith, however, the result is again weakened and finally lifted.

[kap6:34]
The greater so the line of its presentation has to be, so much the more psychologically rightly must the establishment of its tactic be.

[kap6:35]
It was, for example, fundamentally false to make the opponent ridiculous, as this the Austrian and German joke-sheet propaganda above all did. Fundamentally false therefore because the meeting in reality had at once to bring to the man a quite other conviction of the opponent, something which then avenged itself in the most frightful manner; for now the German soldier felt himself, under the immediate impression of the resistance of the opponent, deceived by the makers of his hitherto enlightenment, and in place of a strengthening of his fight-desire or even only firmness the opposite entered. The man despaired.

[kap6:36]
Over against that, the war propaganda of the English and Americans was psychologically right. In that she presented to her own people the German as barbarian and Hun, she prepared the individual soldier already for the horrors of the war and so helped along to preserve him before disappointments. The most frightful weapon which now came to application against him appeared to him only more as the confirmation of his already become enlightenment, and strengthened just as much the belief in the correctness of the assertions of his government as she on the other side heightened rage and hate against the infamous enemy. For the cruel effect of the weapon which he indeed now on himself from

[kap6:37]
the sides of the opponent came to know appeared to him gradually as proof of the "Hunnish" brutality, already known to him, of the barbaric enemy, without that he even for a moment would have been brought so far to reflection that his weapons perhaps, indeed even probably, could work still more frightfully.

[kap6:38]
So the English soldier above all could never feel himself as not-truly instructed from home, what unfortunately with the German was so very much the case that he finally at large everything which came from this side rejected as "swindle" and "cramp." All mere consequences of it that one believed one could detail to propaganda the next-best ass (or even "otherwise" clever man) instead of comprehending that herefor the most genial soul-knowers are just still good enough.

[kap6:39]
So German war propaganda offered an unsurpassable teaching and instruction example for an "enlightenment," in the effects working directly reversed, in consequence of complete lack of every psychologically right consideration.

[kap6:40]
At the opponent, however, infinitely much was to be learned for the one who with open eyes and unlimed feeling worked through for himself the flood-wave, storming on for four and a half years, of hostile propaganda.

[kap6:41]
Worst of all, however, one comprehended the very first presupposition of every propagandistic activity at large: namely the fundamentally subjectively one-sided taking of position of the same to every question worked on by it. On this domain was sinned in a manner, and indeed right from the beginning of the war from above downward, that one indeed received the right to doubt whether so much nonsense could really only be ascribed to pure stupidity.

[kap6:42]
What would one, for example, say over a placard which is to advertise a new soap, thereby, however, also designates other soaps as "good"?

[kap6:43]
One would over it only shake the head.

[kap6:44]
Exactly so the relations are also with political advertising.

[kap6:45]
The task of propaganda is, e.g., not a weighing of the various rights, but the exclusive stressing of the one just to be represented through it. It has not objectively also to research the truth, so far as it is favourable to the others, in order then to set it before the mass in doctrinaire uprightness, but uninterruptedly to serve its own.

[kap6:46]
It was fundamentally false to discuss the guilt at the war from the standpoint that not only Germany alone could be made responsible for

[kap6:47]
the outbreak of this catastrophe; rather it would have been right to burden this guilt restlessly upon the opponent, even if this really had not so corresponded to the true course as it yet now actually was the case. But what was the consequence of this halfness?

[kap6:48]
The broad mass of a people consists not of diplomats or even only state-law teachers, indeed not even of altogether reasonably able-to-judge ones, but of just as wavering as to doubt and unsureness inclined children of men. As soon as through one's own propaganda first once only the shimmer of a right also on the other side is admitted, the ground to the doubt at one's own right is already laid. The mass is not in the position now to distinguish where the foreign injustice ends and its own begins. It becomes in such a case unsure and mistrustful, especially then when the opponent just does not make the same nonsense, but on his side burdens all and every guilt upon the enemy. What is there more explainable than that finally one's own people believes the hostile propaganda, which proceeds more closedly, more unifiedly, even more than its own? And still thereto with a people which anyway suffers so much at the objectivity-fad as the German! For with him now everyone will endeavour only indeed not to do injustice to the enemy, even at the danger of the heaviest burdening, indeed annihilation of his own people and state.

[kap6:49]
That at the authoritative places this naturally is not so thought does not at all come to the consciousness of the mass.

[kap6:50]
Subjective — One-Sided — Unconditional!

[kap6:51]
The people is in its overwhelming majority so femininely disposed and set that less sober consideration than rather feeling-wise sensation determines its thinking and acting.

[kap6:52]
This sensation, however, is not complicated, but very simple and closed. It gives hereby not many differentiations, but a positive or a negative, love or hate, right or injustice, truth or lie, never, however, half so and half so or partly, etc.

[kap6:53]
All that especially the English propaganda has in the truly most genial manner understood — and considered. There there were really no halfnesses which perhaps could have stirred up doubts.

[kap6:54]
The sign for the shining knowledge of the primitivity of the sensation of the broad mass lay in the atrocity propaganda adapted to this condition, which in just as ruthless as genial a manner secured the preconditions for the moral standing at the front, even at greatest actual defeats, as further in the just as striking nailing-fast of the German enemy as the solely guilty part at the outbreak of the war: a lie which only through the unconditional, brazen, one-sided stubbornness with which it was carried forward did account to the feeling-wise, ever extreme attitude of the great people and therefore also was believed.

[kap6:55]
How very this kind of propaganda was effective showed most strikingly the fact that she after four years was not only able to hold the opponent still strictly at the pole, but even began to eat into our own people.

[kap6:56]
That to our propaganda this success was not granted was really not allowed to wonder one. She bore the germ of ineffectiveness already in her inner two-meaningness. Finally it was already in consequence of her content little probable that she would awaken in the masses the necessary impression. To hope that with this flat pacifist dish-water it could succeed to intoxicate men to dying, only our spirit-free "statesmen" managed.

[kap6:57]
So this miserable product was purposeless, indeed even damaging.

Limitation to Persistence

[kap6:60]
But all geniality of the making-up of propaganda will lead to no success if a fundamental principle is not always equally sharply considered. It has to limit itself to little and to repeat this eternally. Persistence is here, as with so much in the world, the first and most important presupposition to success.

[kap6:61]
Precisely on the domain of propaganda one may never let oneself be led by aesthetes or blasé ones: by the first not, because otherwise the content in form and expression in short time, instead of being fit for the mass, develops pulling-force only more for literary tea societies; before the second, however, one guards oneself therefore anxiously, because their lack of own fresh sensation always seeks after new charms. To these people in short time everything becomes nauseating; they wish change and never understand to put themselves into the needs of their not yet so scalded fellow-world or even to comprehend these. They are always the first critics of propaganda or better of its content, which appears to them too old-brought, too hackneyed, then again too outlived, etc. They always want new things, seek change, and become thereby true death-enemies of every effective political mass-winning. For as soon as the organization and the content of a propaganda begin to direct themselves according to their needs, they lose every closedness and flutter apart instead completely.

[kap6:62]
Propaganda, however, is not there to furnish blasé little masters runningly interesting change, but to convince, and indeed to convince the mass. This, however, needs in its sluggishness always a determinate time before she is even only ready to take knowledge of a thing, and only to a thousandfold repetition of simplest concepts will she finally present her memory.

[kap6:63]
Every change may never alter the content of what is to be brought through propaganda, but must always at the close say the same. So the slogan must indeed be illuminated from different sides; alone, the end of every consideration has always anew to lie at the slogan itself. Only so propaganda can and will work unifiedly and closedly.

[kap6:64]
This great line alone, which may never be left, lets at always equal-remaining consequent stressing the final success ripen up.

[kap6:65]
Then, however, one will be able with astonishment to establish to what monstrous, hardly understandable results such a persistence leads.

[kap6:66]
The Hostile War Propaganda

[kap6:67]
Every advertising, be it on the domain of business or of politics, bears the success in the duration and even uniformity of its application.

[kap6:68]
Also here the example of the hostile war propaganda was exemplary: limited to few points of view, exclusively reckoned for the mass, operated with untiring persistence. During the whole war the basic thoughts and forms of execution, once recognized as right, were applied without that even only the least change would ever have been undertaken. It was in the beginning seemingly mad in the brazenness of its assertions, became later unpleasant, and was finally believed. After four and a half years there broke out in Germany a revolution whose slogans stemmed from the hostile war propaganda.

[kap6:69]
In England, however, one comprehended still something: that namely for this spiritual weapon the possible success lies only in the mass of its application; the success, however, richly covers all costs.

[kap6:70]
Propaganda counted there as a weapon of the first rank, while with us it represented the last bread of placeless politicians and little printing-posts of modest heroes. Its success was then also, taken all in all, equal to zero.
