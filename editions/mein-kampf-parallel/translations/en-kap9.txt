[kap9:0]
One day I received from the office superior to me the order to look into what sort of a state of affairs it might be with an apparently political association which, under the name "German Workers' Party," intended in the next days to hold an assembly, and at which also Gottfried Feder was to speak; I would have to go there and look at the association once and then report.

[kap9:1]
The curiosity which on the part of the army was then brought toward the political parties was more than understandable. The revolution had given to the soldier the right of political activity, of which now also precisely the most inexperienced made the richest use. Only in the moment when Centre and Social Democracy to their own sorrow had to recognize that the sympathies of the soldiers began to turn away from the revolutionary parties toward the national movement and re-elevation did one see oneself caused to withdraw the right of election from the troop again and to forbid political activity.

[kap9:2]
That Centre and Marxism seized this measure was illuminating, for had one not undertaken this cutting of the "civic rights" — as one named the political equal-righting of the soldier after the revolution — there would already few years later have been no more November state, but therewith also no further national dishonouring and shame. The troop was then on the best way to create the nation its bloodsuckers and henchmen of the Entente in the interior off the neck. That, however, also the so-called "national" parties voted enthusiastically for the correction of the hitherto views of the November criminals and so helped along to make the instrument of a national elevation harmless showed again whither the ever only doctrinaire conceptions of these most harmless of the harmless are able to lead. This bourgeoisie, really sick with spiritual weakness of age, was in all earnestness of the opinion that the army would become again what it was, namely a ward of German defence-capacity, while Centre and Marxism only thought to break out of her the dangerous

[kap9:3]
national poison-fang, without which now once an army eternally remains police, yet is no troop which is able to fight before the enemy; something which in the following time has indeed proved itself to sufficiency.

[kap9:4]
The "German Workers' Party"

[kap9:5]
Or did our "national politicians" perhaps believe that the development of the army could have been other than a national one? That would look damned similar to these gentlemen, and comes from it when in war one is, instead of a soldier, a chatterer, that is a parliamentarian, and has no more notion what may proceed in the breast of men who remember the mightiest past, once to have been the first soldiers of the world.

[kap9:6]
So I resolved myself to go into the already mentioned assembly of this party, to me up to then likewise still entirely unknown.

[kap9:7]
When in the evening I came into the "Leiberzimmer," later become historical for us, of the former Sterneckerbräu in Munich, I met there about 20 to 25 present, mainly out of the lower layers of the population.

[kap9:8]
The lecture of Feder was already known to me from the courses, so that I could devote myself more to the consideration of the association itself.

[kap9:9]
The impression on me was neither good nor bad; a new founding, like just as many others also. It was precisely then the time in which everyone felt himself called to make up a new party who was not content with the hitherto development and possessed no more trust to the given parties. So these associations shot everywhere only so out of the ground, in order after some time soundlessly and clanglessly to vanish again. The founders possessed mostly no notion of what it means to make out of an association a party or even a movement. So these foundings almost always suffocated of themselves in their ridiculous philistinity.

[kap9:10]
Not otherwise did I judge, after about two hours' listening, the "German Workers' Party." When Feder finally closed I was happy. I had seen enough and already wanted to go, when the now announced free discussion indeed moved me to remain still. Alone also here everything seemed to run off meaninglessly, until suddenly a "professor" came to words, who first doubted at the correctness of the Federian grounds, then, however — after a very good reply of Feder's — suddenly placed himself on the "ground of the facts," not, however, without recommending to the young party in the most urgent manner to take up as an especially important programme point the fight for the "separation"

[kap9:11]
of Bavaria from "Prussia." The man asserted with brazen brow that in this case especially German-Austria would at once join itself to Bavaria, that peace then would become much better, and similar

[kap9:12]
nonsense more. There I then could not do otherwise than also report myself to the word and say to the learned gentleman my opinion over this point — with the success that the gentleman pre-speaker, still before I was finished, left the local like a poured poodle. When I spoke, one had listened with astonished faces, and only when I prepared myself to say good night to the assembly and remove myself did there still come a man sprung after me, present himself (I had not rightly understood the name at all) and press a small booklet, visibly a political brochure, into my hand, with the urgent request to read this indeed.

[kap9:13]
That was very pleasant to me, for now I was allowed to hope perhaps in a simpler manner to get to know the boring association without having to visit still further such interesting assemblies. Besides, this apparent worker had made on me a good impression. Therewith therefore I went.

[kap9:14]
The "Committee Sitting"

[kap9:15]
I lived at that time still in the barracks of the 2nd Infantry Regiment, in a small little room which bore the traces of the revolution still very clearly on itself. In the daytime I was away, mostly at the Rifle Regiment 41 or also in assemblies, at lectures at some other troop part, etc. Only at night I slept in my habitation. Since I every morning, early already before 5 o'clock, was wont to wake up, I had accustomed myself to the play of laying to the little mice, which in the small room drove their entertainment, a couple of little pieces of hard bread-rests or rinds on the floor and now to watch how the droll little animals hunted one another around these couple of delicacies. I had in my life already had so much need that I was able only too well to conceive to myself the hunger and therefore also the pleasure of the little beings.

[kap9:16]
Also on the morning after this assembly I lay toward 5 o'clock awake in the bunk and watched the driving and scurrying. Since I could no more fall asleep, I suddenly remembered the past evening, and now the booklet fell to me which the one worker had given me. So I began to read. It was a small brochure in which the author, just this worker, described how he out of the confusion of Marxist and trade-union phrases came again to national thinking; therefore also the title "My Political Awakening." Since I had first begun, I read the little writing through with interest; reflected itself indeed in it a process which I similarly twelve years before on my own body also had to go through. Involuntarily I saw my own development again become living before me. I thought in the course of the day still some times over the thing and already wanted finally to lay it aside again, when I still not a week later to my astonishment received a postcard of the content that I would be taken up into the "German Workers' Party": I should express myself thereto and therefore on the next Wednesday come to a committee sitting of this party.

[kap9:17]
I was over this kind of "winning" members admittedly more than astonished, and knew not whether I should anger myself over it or laugh thereto. I indeed did not think at all of going to a finished party, but wanted to found my own. This request really did not come into question for me.

[kap9:18]
Already I wanted to let my answer go in writing to the gentlemen, when curiosity conquered and I resolved myself to appear on the laid-fast day in order to lay out my grounds orally.

[kap9:19]
Wednesday came. The inn in which the conscious sitting should take place was the "Old Rosenbad" in the Herrnstraße; a very needy local into which only every holy time someone seemed to go astray. No wonder in the year 1919, since the menu even of the greater eating-houses was able to entice only very modestly and meagrely. This economy, however, I up to there did not know at all.

[kap9:20]
I went through the badly illuminated guest room, in which no man sat, sought the door to the side room, and then had the "session" before me. In the twilight of a half-demolished gas lamp sat at a table four young men, among them also the author of the small brochure, who at once in the most joyful manner greeted me and called me welcome as a new member of the "German Workers' Party."

[kap9:21]
I was now indeed somewhat baffled. Since it was communicated to me that the actual "Reich chairman" first was coming, I wanted also to wait still with my declaration. Finally this one appeared. It was the leading one of the assembly in the Sterneckerbräu on the occasion of the Federian lecture.

[kap9:22]
I had meanwhile become curious again and awaited the things which should come there. Now I at least learned to know the names of the individual gentlemen. The chairman of the "Reich organization" was a Herr Harrer; that of Munich, Anton Drexler.

[kap9:23]
There was now read the protocol of the last sitting, and to the secretary the trust was spoken out. Then the cash report came to the series — there found themselves in the possession of the association in total 7 marks and 50 pfennig — for which the treasurer received the assurance of all-sided trust. This was again brought to protocol. Then there came from the 1st chairman the answers to a letter from Kiel, one from Düsseldorf, and one from Berlin for reading; everyone was agreed with them. Now the incoming was communicated: a letter from Berlin, one from Düsseldorf, and one from Kiel, whose arrival seemed to be taken up with great satisfaction. One declared this rising letter traffic as the best and visible sign of the around-itself-grasping significance of the "German Workers' Party," and then — then there took place a long consultation over the new answers to be given.

[kap9:24]
Frightful, frightful! That was indeed an association-hobby-horsing of the worst kind and manner. Into this club therefore I should enter?

[kap9:25]
Then the new admissions came to speech, that is: my application came to treatment.

[kap9:26]
I now began to ask — yet apart from some guiding principles there was nothing present: no programme, no leaflet, at large nothing printed, no membership cards, indeed not even a miserable stamp — only visibly good belief and good will.

[kap9:27]
To me the smiling had again passed away, for what was this other than the typical sign of the complete helplessness and of the total resignation over all the hitherto parties, their programmes, their intentions, and their activity? What drove these couple of young men together to an outwardly so ridiculous doing was yet only the outflow of their inner voice, which to them, probably more feeling-wise than consciously, let the whole hitherto party-being appear as no more suitable to an elevation of the German nation as well as to the healing of its inner damages. I quickly read through to myself the guiding principles, which lay before in typewriting, and saw also out of them more a seeking than a knowing. Much there was blurred or unclear, some lacked, but nothing was present which could not again have counted as a sign of a wrestling knowledge.

[kap9:28]
What these men felt, that I also knew: it was the longing after a new movement, which should be more than party in the hitherto sense of the word.

[kap9:29]
When on this evening I went again to the barracks, I had already formed my judgment over this association.

[kap9:30]
A Decision for Ever

[kap9:31]
I stood before the probably heaviest question of my life: should I join here, or should I reject?

[kap9:32]
Reason could only advise to rejection; feeling, however, did not let me come to rest, and the oftener I tried to hold before my eyes the senselessness of this whole club, the oftener feeling spoke again for it.

[kap9:33]
In the next days I was restless.

[kap9:34]
I began to consider back and forth. To be politically active I was already long resolved; that this was able to happen only in a new movement was just as clear to me; only the impetus to the deed had up to then always still lacked to me. I did not belong to the men who today begin something in order tomorrow to end again and, if possible, to pass over to a new thing. Precisely this conviction, however, was with the main ground why I was able to resolve myself so heavily to such a new founding, which either had to become everything or else more expediently did not take place at all. I knew that this for me would become a decision for ever, at which a "back" could never be given. For me it was then no passing play, but bloody earnest. I have already then always possessed an instinctive aversion over against men who begin everything without also only carrying out something. These Jacks-of-all-lanes were hateful to me. I held the activity of these people for worse than not-doing.

[kap9:35]
Fate itself seemed now to give me a finger-point. I would never have gone to one of the existing great parties, and shall still lay clear the grounds for it more nearly. This ridiculous small creation with its couple of members seemed to me to possess the one advantage, not yet to have frozen stiff to an "organization," but to leave free to the individual the possibility of a real personal activity. Here one could still work, and the smaller the movement was, so much the sooner she was still to be brought into the right form. Here still the content, the goal, and the way could be determined, which with the existing great parties from the beginning already fell away.

[kap9:36]
The longer I tried to think after, the more there grew in me the conviction that precisely out of such a small movement one day the elevation of the nation could be prepared — never, however, more out of

[kap9:37]
the political parliament parties hanging much too much on old conceptions or even taking part in the use of the new regimen. For what here had to be announced was a new world-view and not a new election parole.

[kap9:38]
Admittedly an infinitely heavy resolve, to want to transform this intention into reality.

[kap9:39]
What preconditions did I then myself bring along to this task?

[kap9:40]
A Nameless One

[kap9:41]
That I was without means and poor seemed to me still the most lightly to be borne; but heavier it was that I now once counted to the nameless ones, was one of the millions whom chance just lets live or calls away again out of existence, without that even only the next surroundings deign to take knowledge thereof. Thereto came still the difficulty which had to result out of my lack of schools.

[kap9:42]
The so-called "intelligence" indeed anyway always looks down with a truly infinite condescension on everyone who was not drawn through the obligatory schools and let the necessary knowledge be pumped into himself. The question indeed never runs: What can the man, but: What has he learned? To these "educated" the greatest hollow-head, if he is only wrapped in sufficient testimonies, counts more than the brightest boy, to whom these costly bags just are lacking. I could therefore easily conceive to myself how this "educated" world would step toward me, and have deceived myself thereby also only insofar as I then held men still for better than they unfortunately in sober reality for the greatest part are. As they are, the exceptions, as everywhere, shine freely all the brighter. I, however, learned thereby ever to distinguish between the eternal pupils and the real able-ones.

[kap9:43]
Member Number Seven

[kap9:44]
After two days' torturing brooding and considering I finally came to the conviction of having to do the step.

[kap9:45]
It was the most decisive resolve of my life.

[kap9:46]
A back could and might no more be given.

[kap9:47]
So I reported myself as a member of the "German Workers' Party" and received a provisional membership certificate with the number: seven.
