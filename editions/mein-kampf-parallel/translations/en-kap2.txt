When the mother died, fate had in one respect already made its decision.

[kap2:1]
In her last months of suffering I had driven to Vienna, to take the entrance examination into the Academy. Equipped with a thick pack of drawings, I had at that time set out on the way, convinced of being able to pass the examination playingly easy. In the Realschule I had already been by far the best draughtsman of my class; since then, my capability had developed itself still quite extraordinarily further, so that my own contentment let me, proud and happy, hope the best.

[kap2:2]
A single clouding sometimes entered: my painterly talent seemed to be surpassed by my draughtsmanly, especially on almost all domains of architecture. Equally, however, my interest in architecture in and for itself also grew ever more. This was still accelerated since I, not yet 16 years old, was allowed for the first time to drive to Vienna for a visit of two weeks. I drove there to study the picture gallery of the Court Museum, but had almost only eyes for the museum itself. I ran the days from early morning until late night from one sight to another; alone, it was always only buildings that fettered me in the first line. For hours I could so stand before the Opera, for hours admire the Parliament; the whole Ringstraße worked upon me like a magic out of A Thousand and One Nights.

[kap2:3]
Capability for Master-Builder

[kap2:4]
Now, then, I was for the second time in the beautiful city, and waited with burning impatience, but also proud confidence, for the result of my entrance examination.

[kap2:5]
I was of the success so convinced that the rejection announced to me hit me like a sudden stroke out of a serene heaven. And yet it was so. As I let myself be presented to the Rector and brought forth the request for explanation of the grounds for my non-admission into the general painting school of the Academy, the gentleman assured me that out of my brought-along drawings my unsuitability for a painter went forth incontestably, but my capability lay apparently on the domain of architecture; for me never the painting school, but only the architecture school of the Academy could come into question. That I had hitherto visited neither a building school nor otherwise received any instruction in architecture, one at first could not understand at all.

[kap2:6]
Beaten, I left the Hansenian splendour-building on the Schillerplatz, for the first time in my young life at odds with myself. For what I had heard about my capability seemed now all at once, like a glaring lightning-flash, to uncover a discord under which I had already long suffered, without hitherto being able to give myself a clear account of the why and wherefore. In a few days I now also knew myself that I would one day become a master-builder.

[kap2:7]
Truly the way was unheard-of heavy; for what I had hitherto missed out of defiance in the Realschule should now take bitter revenge. The visit of the architecture school of the Academy was dependent on the visit of the building school of the Technical School, and the entry into this conditioned a Matura previously taken at a middle school. All this was lacking to me completely. According to human discretion, then, a fulfilment of my artist-dream was no longer possible. As I now, after the death of the mother, for the third time drew to Vienna, and this time for many years, calm and resoluteness had returned with me in the meanwhile elapsed time. The earlier defiance had come again, and my goal was finally taken into the eye. I wanted to become a master-builder, and resistances are not there that one capitulate before them, but that one break them.

[kap2:8]
Five Years of Misery

[kap2:9]
And break I wanted these resistances, always the image of the father before my eyes, who once had wrestled himself upward from a poor village and shoemaker boy to a state official. There my ground was indeed already better, the possibility of the fight so much easier; and what at that time appeared to me as hardness of fate, I praise today as wisdom of providence. In that the Goddess of Need took me into her arms and often threatened to break me, the will to resistance grew, and finally the will remained victor.

[kap2:10]
That I thank to the time of that time, that I have become hard and can be hard. And still more than this I praise it for this, that it tore me loose from the hollowness of the comfortable life, that it drew the mother's boy out of the soft down and gave him Dame Care as a new mother, that it threw the resistant one into the world of misery and poverty, and so let him come to know those for whom he should later fight.

[kap2:11]
*

[kap2:12]
In this time there should also be opened to me the eye for two dangers, which I both formerly scarcely knew by name, in no case, however, grasped in their terrible meaning for the existence of the German people: Marxism and Jewry.

[kap2:13]
Vienna, the city that passes with so many as the epitome of harmless cheerfulness, as festive room of amused men, is for me unfortunately only the living memory of the saddest time of my life.

[kap2:14]
Still today, too, this city can awaken only dull thoughts in me. Five years of misery and lamentation are contained in the name of this Phaeacian city for me. Five years, in which I, first as auxiliary worker, then as small painter, had to earn myself my bread; my truly scanty bread, which yet never sufficed even only to still the common hunger. He was at that time my faithful guardian, who as the only one almost never left me, who in everything honestly shared with me.

[kap2:15]
Formation of the World-View

[kap2:16]
Every book that I acquired excited his participation; a visit of the opera let him then again keep me company for days out; it was a lasting fight with my compassionless friend. And yet I have in this time learned, as never before. Apart from my architecture, the rare, from-the-mouth-saved visit of the opera, I had as the only joy only still books.

[kap2:17]
I read at that time infinitely much, and indeed thoroughly. What so of free time remained to me from my work went completely up for my study. In a few years I so created for myself the foundations of my knowledge, of which I also still today draw.

[kap2:18]
But still more than this.

[kap2:19]
In this time there formed itself in me a world-picture and a world-view, which became the granite foundation of my present acting. I have to learn in addition to what I so once created for myself only little; to change, I needed nothing.

[kap2:20]
On the contrary.

[kap2:21]
I believe firmly today that in general all creative thoughts appear in principle already in youth, in so far as such are present at all. I distinguish between the wisdom of age, which can count only as a greater thoroughness and caution as the result of the experiences of a long life, and the geniality of youth, which in inexhaustible fruitfulness pours out thoughts and ideas, without at first being able even to work them up, as a consequence of the fullness of their number. It delivers the building materials and future plans, from which the wiser age takes the stones, hews them, and raises the building, in so far as the so-called wisdom of age has not stifled the geniality of youth.

*

[kap2:23]
The life that I had led until then in the paternal house distinguished itself indeed little or in nothing from that of all the others. Free of care I could expect the new day, and a social problem there was for me not.

[kap2:24]
Casting Off Petty-Bourgeois Blinkers

[kap2:25]
The surroundings of my youth composed themselves out of the circles of small bourgeoisdom, thus out of a world that possesses only very few relations to the pure hand-worker. For as strange as it may also seem at the first glance, so is yet the cleft just between these, in no way economically brilliantly placed layers and the worker of the fist often deeper than one thinks. The ground of this, let us say almost enmity, lies in the fear of a societal group that has lifted itself only a very short time ago out of the level of the hand-workers, of sinking back again into the old, little esteemed stand, or at least of still being counted to it. To this there comes still with many the disgusting memory of the cultural misery of this lower class, the frequent roughness of the intercourse among one another, in which the own, even so small a standing in societal life lets every touch with this overcome culture- and life-step appear as an unbearable burden.

[kap2:26]
So it comes that frequently the higher-standing descends more impartially to his lowest fellow man than it appears possible even to the "up-climber."

[kap2:27]
For up-climber is now once everyone who by his own energy wrestles himself upward out of a previous life-standing into a higher one.

[kap2:28]
Finally, however, this frequently very harsh fight lets pity die off. The own painful wrestling for existence kills the feeling for the misery of those left behind.

[kap2:29]
With me, fate possessed mercy in this respect. In that it forced me to return again into this world of poverty and insecurity, which the father once in the course of his life had already left, it drew the blinkers of a limited petty-bourgeois education from my eyes. Now first I learned to know men; learned to distinguish between hollow appearance or brutal exterior and their inner essence.

[kap2:30]
Social Oppositions of Vienna

[kap2:31]
Vienna belonged, after the turn of the century, already to the socially most unfavourable little cities.

[kap2:32]
Shining riches and repellent poverty relieved one another in abrupt change. In the centre and in the inner districts one felt so rightly the pulse-beat of the 52-million-Reich, with all the questionable magic of the nationalities-state. The Court in its blinding splendour worked similar to a magnet on riches and intelligence of the rest of the state. To this there came still the strong centralisation of the Habsburg monarchy in and for itself.

[kap2:33]
In it there offered itself the only possibility to hold this peoples-mash together in firm form. The consequence of this, however, was an extraordinary concentration of high and highest offices in the capital and residence city.

[kap2:34]
But Vienna was not only politically and spiritually the central of the old Danube monarchy, but also economically. To the army of high officers, state officials, artists and scholars there stood opposite a still greater army of workers; to the riches of the aristocracy and of trade, a bloody poverty. Before the palaces of the Ringstraße thousands of unemployed loafed, and under this via triumphalis of old Austria there housed in the twilight and mud of the canals the homeless. Hardly in a German city was the social question better to be studied than in Vienna. But one should not deceive oneself. This "studying" cannot happen from above down. Who does not himself find himself in the clamps of this throttling serpent learns never to know her poison-fangs. In the other case there comes out nothing but superficial chatter or lying sentimentality. Both are of harm. The one, because it can never press to the kernel of the problem; the other, because it goes past it. I do not know what is more devastating: the non-observation of social need, as the majority of those favoured by luck, or also raised through own merit, lets be seen daily, or that just as high-nosed as sometimes again importunately tactless, but always gracious condescension of certain "with the people feeling" fashion-women in skirts and trousers. These men sin in any case more than they in their instinctless understanding are in general even able to grasp.

[kap2:35]
The Auxiliary Worker

[kap2:36]
Hence to their own astonishment the result of a social "disposition" practised by them is always null, frequently, however, even indignant rejection; which then counts, truly, as proof of the thanklessness of the people.

[kap2:37]
That a social activity has nothing at all to do with that, above all may raise no claim to thanks at all, since it is indeed not to distribute graces but to restore rights, dawns only unwillingly on such a kind of heads.

[kap2:38]
I was preserved from learning the social question in such a manner. In that it drew me into the spell-circle of its suffering, it seemed not to invite me to "learning," but rather to want to prove itself on me myself. It was not its merit that the rabbit nevertheless survived the operations whole and healthy.

[kap2:39]
*

[kap2:40]
If I now want to try to reproduce the series of my sensations of that time today, then this can never be even approximately complete; only the most essential and for me often most shattering impressions shall here be presented, with the few teachings as I already drew them in this time.

[kap2:41]
It mostly did not become very hard for me at that time to find work in itself, since I was indeed not a learned craftsman, but only as a so-called auxiliary worker, and many a time as a casual worker, had to try to create my daily bread for myself.

[kap2:42]
I placed myself thereby on the standpoint of all those who shake the dust of Europe from their feet, with the unrelenting resolve to found for themselves in the New World also a new existence, to conquer a new home. Loosed from all hitherto paralysing representations of calling and stand, of surroundings and tradition, they now grasp after every earnings that offers itself to them, tackle every work, so working themselves through ever more to the conception,

[kap2:43]
that honest work never disgraces, entirely equal of whatever kind it may also be.

[kap2:44]
The Insecurity of the Bread-Earnings

[kap2:45]
So I too was resolved to jump with both feet into the world new for me and to fight myself through.

[kap2:46]
That there is always some work there, I soon learned to know; alone, just as quickly also how easily it is to be lost again.

[kap2:47]
The insecurity of the daily bread-earnings appeared to me in a short time as one of the heaviest shadow-sides of the new life.

[kap2:48]
Indeed the "learned" worker will not be set on the street so frequently as is the case with the unlearned; alone entirely is he, too, not protected from this fate. With him there enters, in place of the bread-loss out of lack of work, the lock-out or his own strike.

[kap2:49]
Here the insecurity of the daily earnings already takes bitter revenge on the whole economy itself.

[kap2:50]
The peasant lad who wanders into the metropolis, attracted by the supposedly or indeed also really lighter work, the shorter working time, most of all, however, through the blinding light that the metropolis now once is able to radiate forth, is still accustomed to a certain security of the earnings. He tends to leave the old post only when a new one stands at least in prospect. Finally the lack of land-workers is great; the probability of a longer lack of work thus in and for itself very small. It is now an error to believe that the young lad betaking himself into the metropolis would be carved from the outset out of worse wood than the one also further honestly nourishing himself on the peasant soil. No, on the contrary: experience shows that all emigrating elements consist rather of the healthiest and most energetic natures than, say, reversed. To these "emigrants," however, counts not only the America-wanderer, but also already the young farmhand who resolves to leave the home village to draw to the foreign metropolis.

[kap2:51]
The Fate of the Worker

[kap2:52]
Also he is ready to take an uncertain fate upon himself. Mostly he comes with some money into the great city, needs therefore not already to despair on the first day if the misfortune lets him find no work for a longer time. Worse, however, it becomes when he loses a found place of work again in a short time. The finding of a new one is, especially in winter, frequently hard, if not impossible. The first weeks it still goes. He receives unemployment support out of the funds of his trade union and fights through as well as just possible. Alone, when the last own heller and pfennig is used up, the fund, as a consequence of the long duration of the unemployment, also stops the support, then comes the great need. Now he loafs around hungering, pawns and sells often still the last, comes so in his clothing ever more down, and sinks thereby also outwardly into surroundings that now poison him, to the bodily misfortune, still spiritually. If he then becomes homeless as well, and this is (as it often tends to be the case) in winter, then the misery becomes already very great. Finally he finds again some work. Alone, the game repeats itself. A second time it hits him similarly; a third time perhaps still more heavily, so that he learns to endure the eternally uncertain gradually more indifferently. Finally the repetition becomes habit.

[kap2:53]
So the otherwise diligent man loosens in his whole conception of life, in order gradually to ripen toward the instrument of those who make use of him only for the sake of low advantages. He was so often without own fault unemployed that it now, on once more or less, also does not matter, even when it thereby no longer concerns the fighting-out of economic rights, but the destroying of state, societal, or generally cultural values. He will, if not already strike-glad, yet be strike-indifferent. This process I could follow on a thousand examples with open eyes.

[kap2:54]
The longer I saw the game, the more my aversion grew against the million-city, which first greedily drew men to itself, in order then to grind them so cruelly.

[kap2:55]
When they came, they counted still always to their people; when they stayed, they were lost to it.

[kap2:56]
Also I was so thrown around by life in the world-city, and could thus on my own body prove the workings of this fate, and

[kap2:57]
go through them in soul. I saw there still one thing: the fast change from work to non-work and back, as well as the eternally thereby conditioned swinging of the in- and out-come, destroys in the duration, with many, the feeling for thrift, just as the understanding for a wise life-division. The body seemingly accustoms itself slowly to living in good times out of the full, and in bad to hunger. Yes, hunger throws over every resolve for later reasonable division in the better time of the earnings, in that it gauches before the one tormented by it, in a lasting Fata Morgana, the pictures of a sated well-living, and understands how to heighten this dream to such a longing that such a sick desire becomes the end of every self-restriction, as soon as earnings and wages in any way permit this. Hence it comes that the man who has scarcely attained a work immediately forgets, in the most unreasonable manner, every division, in order instead to live out of full draughts into the day. This leads even to the overturning of the small weekly household, since even here the wise division fails to appear; it still suffices at first for five days instead of for seven, later only more for three, finally for hardly still one day, in order at the close to be revelled away already in the first night. At home are then often wife and children. Sometimes they too are infected by this life, especially when the man in and for himself is good to them, indeed even, in his manner and way, lifts them. Then the weekly wage is in two, three days at home commonly used up; there is eaten and drunk so long as the money holds, and the last days are just as commonly hungered through.

[kap2:58]
Then the wife steals into the neighbourhood and surroundings, borrows a little out, makes small debts at the grocer's, and seeks so to hold out through the bad last days of the week. At midday they all sit together before meagre bowls, sometimes also before nothing, and wait for the coming pay-day, talk of him, make plans, and while they hunger, they dream already again of the coming luck.

[kap2:59]
So the small children are made familiar with this misery in their earliest youth.

[kap2:60]
Badly, however, it ends when the man from the beginning goes his own ways and the wife, just for the children's sake, steps up against it. Then there is strife and contention, and in the measure in which the man now becomes stranger to the wife, he comes nearer to alcohol. Every Saturday he is now drunk, and in the self-preservation drive for herself and her children the wife fights herself for the

[kap2:61]
few groschen that she must hunt down from him, mostly still on the way from the factory to the dive. If he finally on Sunday or Monday night himself comes home, drunk and brutal, always, however, freed from the last heller and pfennig, then scenes often play themselves out that God have mercy.

[kap2:62]
In hundreds of examples I have lived through all this, at first disgusted or indeed also indignant, in order later to grasp the whole tragedy of this suffering, to understand the deeper causes. Unhappy victims of bad conditions.

[kap2:63]
Almost still duller at that time were the dwelling conditions; the dwelling-misery of the Viennese auxiliary worker was a terrible one. I shudder still today when I think on these lamentable dwelling-caves, on hostel and mass quarters, on these dull pictures of filth, disgusting dirt, and worse.

[kap2:64]
How must it, and how must it once become, when out of these misery-caves the stream of loosed slaves pours itself over the other, so thoughtless co-world and fellow men!

[kap2:65]
For thoughtless is this other world.

[kap2:66]
The Way to Betterment

[kap2:67]
Thoughtlessly it lets things just drift, without in its instinctlessness even to suspect that sooner or later fate must proceed to retribution, if men do not in time still reconcile fate. How grateful I am today to that providence which let me go into this school. In it I could no longer sabotage what did not please me. It educated me quickly and thoroughly.

[kap2:68]
If I did not want to despair on the men of my surroundings of that time, I had to learn to distinguish between their outer essence and life and the grounds of their development. Only then could all this be endured without having to lose heart. Then there grew out of all the misfortune and misery, out of filth and outer decay, no longer men, but sad results of sad laws; in which the heaviness of my own, yet not lighter life-fight preserved me from now, say, capitulating in lamentable sentimentality before the decayed final products of this development-process.

[kap2:69]
No, so shall this not be understood.

[kap2:70]
Already at that time I saw that here only a double way could lead to the goal of a betterment of these conditions:

[kap2:71]
Deepest social feeling of responsibility for the production of better foundations of our development, paired with brutal resoluteness in the breaking-down of incorrigible excrescences.

[kap2:72]
Just as nature concentrates her greatest attention not on the preservation of the existing, but on the breeding of the offspring, as the bearer of the kind, so it can also in human life less concern itself with artificially ennobling existing bad, which with the disposition of man is to ninety-nine percent impossible, than with securing to a coming development healthier paths from the beginning on.

[kap2:73]
The Essence of Social Activity

[kap2:74]
Already during my Viennese existence-fight it had become clear to me that social activity never and nevermore has to behold its task in just as ridiculous as purposeless welfare-drowsinesses, but rather in the removal of such fundamental defects in the organisation of our economic and cultural life as must lead to degenerations of individuals, or at least can tempt to them.

[kap2:75]
The difficulty of the proceeding with last and most brutal means against the state-hostile criminaldom lies indeed not least just in the insecurity of the judgment over the inner motives or causes of such time-appearances.

[kap2:76]
This insecurity is only too grounded in the feeling of an own guilt at such tragedies of decay; it paralyses, however, now every earnest and firm resolve, and so helps at the, because wavering, also weak and half execution even of the most necessary measures of self-preservation.

[kap2:77]
Only when once a time is no longer haunted by the shadows of its own consciousness of guilt does it receive, with the inner calm, also the outer force brutally and ruthlessly to cut out the wild shoots, to weed out the weeds.

[kap2:78]
Since the Austrian state knew a social jurisdiction and legislation in general as good as not at all, its weakness in the combat against even bad excrescences was also eye-leaping great.

[kap2:79]
*

[kap2:80]
I do not know what now at this time horrified me most: the economic misery of my companions of that time, the ethical and moral roughness, or the low stand of their spiritual culture.

[kap2:81]
The Lack of "National Pride"

[kap2:82]
How often does not our bourgeoisdom rise up in all moral indignation when it hears out of the mouth of some lamentable tramp the utterance that it remains equal to him whether he be a German or also not, that he feel himself equally well everywhere, in so far as he only have his needful livelihood.

[kap2:83]
This lack of "national pride" is then lamented in the deepest, and vigorous expression given to the abhorrence before such a disposition.

[kap2:84]
How many, however, have already laid before themselves the question of what then actually forms with them themselves the cause of their better disposition?

[kap2:85]
How many indeed grasp the unnumbered individual memories of the greatness of the fatherland, of the nation, on all domains of cultural and artistic life, which mediate to them as a collective result just the justified pride of being allowed to be members of a people so graced?

[kap2:86]
How many suspect, indeed, how very the pride on the fatherland is dependent on the knowledge of the greatness of the same on all these domains?

[kap2:87]
Do now our bourgeois circles think over in what ridiculous extent this presupposition to pride on the fatherland is mediated to the "people"?

[kap2:88]
One should not talk oneself out on this, that in "other countries this is indeed also not otherwise," the worker there, however, "nevertheless" stood to his folkdom. Even if this were so, it would not be able to serve as excuse of own omissions. It is, however, not so. For what we always designate with a "chauvinistic" education, e.g. of the French people, is yet nothing other than the excessive lifting-out of the greatness of France on all domains of culture, or, as the Frenchman likes to say, of "civilisation." The young Frenchman is indeed not educated to objectivity, but to the most subjective view that one can only think of, in so far as it concerns the meaning of the political or cultural greatness of his fatherland.

[kap2:89]
The Suffering-Way of the Worker-Child

[kap2:90]
This education will thereby always have to limit itself to general, quite great standpoints, which, if needful, in eternal repetition are to be impressed upon the memory and the feeling of the people.

[kap2:91]
Now, however, with us there comes to the negative sin of omission still the positive destruction of the little that the individual has the luck to learn in school. The rats of the political poisoning of our people eat also this little still out of the heart and the memory of the broad mass, in so far as need and misery have not already provided for their part.

[kap2:92]
One should just picture to oneself the following:

[kap2:93]
In a cellar dwelling, consisting out of two dull rooms, there houses a seven-headed worker family. Among the five children also a boy of, let us assume, three years. It is this the age in which the first impressions come to a child's consciousness. With gifted ones there are found still into high age traces of the memory out of this time. Already the narrowness and overfilling of the room does not lead to favourable conditions. Strife and contention will very frequently already arise in this manner. Men live so not with one another, but press upon one another. Every, even the smallest, dispute, which in a roomier dwelling already through a light separating can be balanced, so resolves itself again of its own accord, leads here to a no longer ending, disgusting strife. With the children this is naturally still bearable; they fight in such conditions indeed always, and forget it among one another again quickly and thoroughly. When this fight is fought out under the parents themselves, and indeed almost every day, in forms that in inner roughness often really leave nothing to be wished, then the results of such a lesson-by-viewing must, even if ever so slowly, finally show themselves with the little ones. Of what kind they must be, when this mutual discord takes the form of raw excesses of the father against the mother, leads to maltreatments in a drunken state, can be imagined only with difficulty by one who does not know such a milieu.

[kap2:94]
Young Authority-Despisers

[kap2:95]
With six years the small, to-be-pitied boy suspects things before which even an adult can feel only horror. Morally poisoned-at, bodily undernourished, the poor little head lousy, so the young "state-citizen" wanders into the elementary school. That it with ach and groan comes to reading and writing is also just about all. Of a learning at home there can be no speech. On the contrary. Mother and father talk indeed themselves, and indeed toward the children, in a manner not to be reproduced, about teacher and school; are much more ready to say grossnesses to those than, say, to lay their little sprout over the knee and bring him to reason. What the little fellow otherwise still hears at home also does not lead to a strengthening of the respect before the dear co-world. Nothing good is here left on humanity; no institution remains unattacked; from the teacher begun up to the summit of the state. Whether it concern religion or morality in itself, the state or society, all the same, everything is reviled, in the most unseemly manner dragged into the dirt of a lowest disposition. When the young man is now with fourteen years dismissed out of school, it is already hard to decide any more what is greater on him: the unbelievable stupidity, in so far as it concerns real knowledge and capability, or the corroding impudence of his bearing, bound with an immorality already in this age at which one's hair could stand on end.

[kap2:96]
What standing, however, can this man, to whom now already hardly anything is holy any more, who has just as much learned to know nothing great as he, reversely, suspects and knows every low-ness of life, take in life, into which he indeed now prepares to step out?

[kap2:97]
Out of the three-year child there has become a fifteen-year despiser of every authority. The young man has come into touch only with dirt and filth, and has learned to know still nothing that could stimulate him to some higher enthusiasm.

[kap2:98]
The Presupposition of the "Nationalisation"

[kap2:99]
Now, however, he comes first still into the high school of this existence.

[kap2:100]
Now the same life sets in that he had taken into himself from the father along the years of childhood. He idles around and comes God knows when home, thrashes for alternation also himself still the torn-together being that once was his mother, curses over God and the world, and is finally, out of some particular occasion, condemned and brought into a youth prison.

[kap2:101]
There he receives the last finish.

[kap2:102]
The dear bourgeois co-world, however, is quite astonished over the lacking "national enthusiasm" of this young "state-citizen."

[kap2:103]
It sees how in theatre and cinema, in trash literature and filth press, day for day the poison is poured bucket-wise into the people, and is then astonished over the small "moral content," the "national indifference," of the masses of this people. As if cinema kitsch, trash press, and the like would give the foundations of the knowledge of fatherlandly greatness. From the earlier education of the individual quite apart.

[kap2:104]
What I formerly had never suspected, I learned at that time to understand quickly and thoroughly:

[kap2:105]
The question of the "nationalisation" of a people is, in the first line, also a question of the creation of healthy social conditions as the foundation of an education-possibility of the individual. For only who, through education and school, learns to know the cultural, economic, above all, however, political greatness of his own fatherland, is able, and will also gain, that inner pride of being allowed to be a member of such a people. And fight I can only for something that I love; love only what I respect; and respect what I at least know.

[kap2:106]

[kap2:107]
In so far as my interest for the social question was awakened, I also began to study it with all thoroughness. It was a new, hitherto unknown world that so opened itself to me.

[kap2:108]
Draughtsman and Water-Colourist

[kap2:109]

My present work ran indeed parallel to my future calling. Also I could now, as master of my own time, divide this essentially better for myself than was possible earlier.

[kap2:110]

[kap2:111]

Also here I therefore read and learned much.

The Art of Reading

[kap2:116]
Truly, I understand under "reading" perhaps something other than the great average of our so-called "intelligence."

[kap2:117]
I know men who "read" infinitely much, and indeed book for book, letter about letter, and whom I yet would not like to designate as "well-read." They possess truly a monstrous amount of "knowledge"; alone their brain does not understand how to carry out a division and registry of this material taken into itself. There lacks to them the art, in the book, to separate the valuable for them from the valueless; the one then to keep in the head for ever; the other, if possible, not to see at all; in any case, however, not to drag it along as purposeless ballast. Also reading is indeed not an end in itself, but a means to such. It shall in the first line help along to fill the frame that disposition and capability draw for everyone; consequently it shall deliver tools and building materials that the individual needs in his life-calling, entirely equal whether this serves only the primitive bread-earning or represents the satisfaction of a higher determination; in the second line, however, it shall mediate a general world-picture. In both cases it is, however, necessary that the content of what is read at the time be handed over to the memory for keeping not in the sequence of the book, or even of the book-sequence, but as a mosaic little stone in the general world-picture receives its place at the position coming to it, and so just helps along to form this picture in the head of the reader. In the other case there arises a confused jumble of learned-in stuff, which is just as valueless as, on the other hand, it makes the unhappy possessor conceited. For this one now believes in all earnest to be "educated," to understand something of life, to possess knowledge, while he with every new growth of this kind of "education" in truth estranges himself from the world more and more, until he not seldom ends either in a sanatorium or as a "politician" in a parliament. Never will it succeed to such a head to fetch out, from the jumble of his "knowledge," the fitting for the demand of an hour, since indeed his spiritual ballast does not lie ordered in the lines of life, but in the sequence of the books, as he read them and as their content now sits in his head. If fate at its demands of daily life would always remind him of the right application of what was once read, then it would have to

[kap2:118]
but also still mention book and page number, since the poor dolt otherwise in all eternity would not find the right. Since it now, however, does not do this, these nine-times clever ones get at every critical hour into the most terrible embarrassment, seek crampedly after analogous cases, and catch, with deadly certainty, naturally the false recipes.

[kap2:119]
If it were not so, one could not grasp the political performances of our learned government heroes in highest places, unless one resolved, instead of pathological disposition, to assume villainous baseness.

[kap2:120]
Who, however, has the art of right reading within him, him the feeling, at the studying of every book, every journal, or brochure, will instantaneously make attentive to all that which in his opinion is suited for him for lasting retention, because either purposeful or generally worth knowing. As soon as what is so gained finds its sense-fitting incorporation into the always already somehow present picture that the representation of this or that thing has created for itself, it will work either correcting or supplementing, thus either raising the rightness or distinctness of the same. If now life suddenly lays some question before for examination or answering, then at such a kind of reading the memory will instantaneously grasp to the measure of the already present viewing-picture and fetch out of it all the individual contributions concerning these questions, collected in decades, submit them to the understanding for examination and new inspection, until the question is cleared or answered.

[kap2:121]
Only so has reading then sense and purpose.

[kap2:122]
A speaker, for example, who does not on such a manner deliver to his understanding the necessary foundations, will never be in the position, at contradiction, compellingly to represent his view, may it also a thousand times correspond to truth or reality. At every discussion the memory will basely leave him in the stitch: he will find neither grounds for the hardening of what is asserted by him, nor such for the refutation of the opponent. So long as it thereby, as with a speaker, in the first line concerns only the disgrace of his own person, this may still pass; bad, however, it becomes when fate appoints such a much-knower but nothing-can-er as leader of a state.

[kap2:123]
I have since early youth striven to read in the right manner, and was thereby supported in the happiest manner by memory and understanding. And in

[kap2:124]
such a sense regarded, the Vienna time was for me especially fruitful and valuable. The experiences of daily life formed the stimulation to ever new study of the most different problems. In that I finally so was in the position to ground reality theoretically, to test theory on reality, I was preserved from either suffocating in theory or going shallow in reality.

[kap2:125]
So in this time, in two most important questions, apart from the social, the experience of daily life became determining and stimulating for the most thorough theoretical study.

[kap2:126]
Who knows when I would once have deepened myself into the teachings and the essence of Marxism, if the time of that time had not formally thrust me with the head upon this problem!

The Social Democracy

[kap2:128]
What I in my youth knew of the Social Democracy was heartily little and richly incorrect.

[kap2:129]
That it led the fight for the general and secret right to vote pleased me inwardly. For my understanding told me already at that time that this would have to lead to a weakening of the Habsburg regime so hated by me. In the conviction that the Danube state, except under sacrifice of Germandom, could yet never be held, that, however, even the price of a slow Slavisation of the German element would by no means have meant the guarantee of a then also really viable Reich, since the state-preserving force of Slavdom must be assessed as highly doubtful, I greeted every development that in my conviction had to lead to the collapse of this impossible state, condemning Germandom in ten million men to death. The more the languages-tohuwabohu also ate through and tore apart the Parliament, the more the hour of the decay of this Babylonian Reich had to approach, and with it, however, also the hour of the freedom of my German-Austrian people. Only so could then one day the connection to the old motherland come again.

[kap2:130]
So this activity of the Social Democracy was therefore not unsympathetic to me. That it finally, as my harmless disposition of that time was still stupid enough to believe, strove to lift the life-conditions of the worker, seemed to me likewise rather to speak for it than against it. What repelled me most was its hostile standing toward the fight for the preservation of Germandom, the lamentable courting for the favour of the Slavic "comrades," who received this love-courting, in so far as it was bound with practical concessions, indeed, otherwise, however, held themselves back arrogantly high-nosed, giving the importunate beggars in this manner the deserved wage.

[kap2:131]
So, at the age of seventeen years, the word "Marxism" was to me still little known, while "Social Democracy" and socialism appeared to me as identical concepts. It needed also here first the fist of fate to open my eye over this most unheard-of peoples-deception.

First Encounter with Social Democrats

[kap2:133]
If I had until then learned to know the social-democratic party only as a spectator at some mass demonstrations, without possessing even the slightest insight into the mentality of its followers or even into the essence of the teaching, I now came in one stroke into touch with the products of its education and "world-view." And what otherwise perhaps would have entered only after decades, I received now in the course of a few months: the understanding for a pestilence wandering under the larva of social virtue and neighbour-love, from which may mankind as quickly as possible free the earth, since otherwise the earth could easily become free of mankind.

[kap2:134]
At the building site my first encounter with Social Democrats took place.

[kap2:135]
It was from the beginning not very pleasing. My clothing was still somewhat in order, my speech cared-for, and my essence reserved. I would perhaps not have troubled myself about my new surroundings at all, if not already on the third or fourth day an event had entered that forced me immediately to a taking of a position. After fourteen days I then, however, could no longer, even if I otherwise had still wanted. The First Terror

[kap2:138]
The first days I was vexed.

[kap2:139]
At midday a part went into the nearest lying inns, while another remained at the building place and there consumed a mostly very meagre midday meal. It was these the married ones, to whom their wives in miserable dishes brought the midday soup. Toward the end of the week this number became ever greater; why, I grasped only later.

[kap2:140]
Now there was politicising.

[kap2:141]
I drank my bottle of milk and ate my piece of bread somewhere sideways, and cautiously studied my new surroundings, or thought over my miserable lot. Nevertheless I heard more than enough; also it often seemed to me as if one deliberately moved up to me, in order so perhaps to cause me to a taking of position. In any case, what I so heard was suited to incite me to the outermost. One rejected there everything: the nation, as an invention of the "capitalist" — how often did I have to hear only alone this word! — classes; the fatherland, as an instrument of the bourgeoisie for the exploitation of the workerdom; the authority of the law as a means for the suppression of the proletariat; the school, as an institute for the breeding of the slave material, but also of the slave-holders; religion, as a means of the stupefaction of the people determined for exploitation; morality, as a sign of stupid sheep-patience, etc. There was there, however, purely nothing at all that was not drawn into the filth of a terrible depth.

[kap2:142]
At first I tried to be silent. Finally it went no longer. I began to take position, began to contradict. Then I had indeed to recognise that this was completely without prospect so long as I did not possess at least certain knowledges over the now once contested points. So I began to scent in the sources from which they drew their supposed wisdom. Book upon book, brochure upon brochure, now came to the series.

[kap2:143]
At the building site, however, it now often went hotly. I disputed, from day to day better informed also over their own knowledge than my contradictors themselves, until one day that means came to application which truly defeats reason the easiest: the terror, the violence. Some of the word-leaders of the counter-side forced me either to leave the building site immediately or from the

[kap2:144]
scaffold to fly down. Since I was alone, resistance appeared without prospect; I preferred, richer by one experience, to follow the first advice.

[kap2:145]
I went, filled with disgust, but at the same time also so seized that it would have been quite impossible for me now to turn my back on the whole affair. No; after the shooting-up of the first indignation, obstinacy won the upper hand again. I was firmly resolved nevertheless to go again onto a building site. I was strengthened in this resolve still through the need which, some weeks later, after the consuming of the small saved wage, closed me in its heartless arms. Now I had to, whether I wanted or not. And the game then also went again from the front loose, in order to end similarly as at the first time.

[kap2:146]
At that time I wrestled in my inner: Are these still men, worth to belong to a great people?

[kap2:147]
A tormenting question; for if it is answered with yes, then the fight for a folkdom is really no longer worth the troubles and sacrifices that the best have to bring for such an excrescence; if the answer, however, is no, then our people is already poor in men.

The Social-Democratic Press

[kap2:149]
With restless oppression I saw in such days of pondering and boring-in the mass of those no longer to be counted to their people swell to a threatening army.

[kap2:150]
With what other feelings I now stared into the endless rows of four of a mass demonstration of Viennese workers taking place one day! Almost two hours long I stood so there and observed with held breath the enormous human dragon-worm that there slowly rolled past. In anxious dejectedness I finally left the place and wandered homeward. On the way I caught sight in a tobacco shop of the "Arbeiterzeitung," the central organ of the old Austrian Social Democracy. In a cheap peoples-café, into which I often went in order to read newspapers, it also lay out; alone I could hitherto not bring it over me to look into the miserable sheet, whose whole tone worked on me like spiritual vitriol, longer than two minutes. Under the depressing impression of the demonstration, an inner voice now drove me to buy the sheet once and then to read it thoroughly. In the evening I provided for this then also, under overcoming of the sudden rage sometimes rising in me over this concentrated solution of lies. More than out of all theoretical literature I could now, out of the daily reading of the social-democratic press, study the inner essence of these thought-courses.

[kap2:151]
For what a difference between the phrases of freedom, beauty, and dignity shimmering in the theoretical literature, the will-o'-the-wisping word-tangle expressing seemingly deepest wisdom laboriously, the disgustingly humane morality — everything written down with the iron brow of a prophetic certainty — and the brutal daily press of this salvation-teaching of the new mankind, shrinking back before no baseness, working with every means of slander and a truly beam-bending virtuosity of lying! The one is determined for the stupid dupes out of middle and naturally also higher "intelligence-layers," the other for the mass.

The Psyche of the Mass

[kap2:153]
For me, the deepening into literature and press of this teaching and organisation meant the finding-again to my people.

[kap2:154]
What first appeared to me as an unbridgeable cleft should now become the occasion to a greater love than ever before.

[kap2:155]
Only a fool, with knowledge of this enormous work of poisoning, is able also still to damn the victim. The more I in the next years made myself independent, the more there grew, with rising distance, the glance for the inner causes of the social-democratic successes. Now I grasped the meaning of the brutal demand to hold only red newspapers, to visit only red assemblies, to read red books, etc. In plastic clarity I saw the compelling result of this teaching of intolerance before my eyes. The psyche of the broad mass is not receptive for everything half and weak.

[kap2:156]
Equal to the woman, whose soul-feeling is determined less through grounds of abstract reason than through such of an indefinable, feeling-like longing after supplementing force, and who therefore bows herself rather to the strong than dominates the weakling, so also the mass lifts more the ruler than the beggar, and feels itself inwardly more satisfied through a teaching that tolerates no other beside itself than through the granting of liberal freedom; it also mostly knows how to begin only little with it, and feels itself even easily abandoned. The impertinence of its spiritual terrorisation comes just as little to its consciousness as the outrageous mishandling of its human freedom; it suspects, indeed, the inner madness of the whole teaching in no manner. So it sees only the ruthless force and brutality of its goal-conscious utterances, to which it finally always bows.

[kap2:157]
If to the Social Democracy a teaching of better truthfulness but equal brutality of the carrying-through is set opposite, this will conquer, even if after the heaviest fight.

The Tactics of the Social Democracy

[kap2:159]
Before even two years had passed, both the teaching and the technical tool of the Social Democracy was clear to me.

[kap2:160]
I grasped the infamous spiritual terror that this movement exercises above all on the bourgeoisdom grown neither morally nor in soul to such attacks, in that it, on a given sign, always lets a formal drumfire of lies and slanders rattle down against the opponent appearing most dangerous to it, so long until the nerves of those attacked break, and they, in order only to have quiet again, sacrifice the hated one.

[kap2:161]
Alone, the quiet these fools nevertheless do not receive.

[kap2:162]
The game begins anew, and is so often repeated until the fear before the wild cur becomes suggestive paralysis.

[kap2:163]
Since the Social Democracy knows the value of force out of its own experience the best, it also runs most storm against those in whose essence it scents something of this in any case so rare material. Reversely, it praises every weakling of the other side, now cautiously, now louder, according to the recognised or suspected spiritual quality.

[kap2:164]
It fears an impotent, will-less genius less than a force-nature, even if of modest spirit.

[kap2:165]
Most insistently it recommends weaklings in spirit and force together. It understands how to awaken the appearance as if only so quiet were to be preserved, while it thereby in clever caution, but nevertheless unswervingly, conquers one position after the other, now through quiet extortion, now through actual theft in moments when the general attention is turned to other things, either does not want to be disturbed or holds the affair for too small to excite great sensation and to provoke the evil opponent anew.

[kap2:166]
It is a tactic found under exact calculation of all human weaknesses, whose result must almost mathematically lead to success, if just the counter-side does not also learn to fight against poison gas with poison gas. To weakling natures it must thereby be said that it here just concerns being or not-being.

[kap2:167]
Not less understandable became to me the meaning of the bodily terror toward the individual, toward the mass.

[kap2:168]
Also here exact calculation of the psychological working.

[kap2:169]
The terror at the place of work, in the factory, in the assembly local, and on the occasion of mass demonstrations, will always be accompanied by success, so long as there does not step opposite to it an equally great terror.

[kap2:170]
Then truly the party will whine in terrible screaming, "Zeter and Mordio," will, as an old despiser of every state authority, shriekingly call after this one, in order in most cases in the general confusion actually to reach the goal — namely: it will find the horned cattle of a higher official, who, in the imbecilic hope of perhaps thereby making the feared opponent inclined to himself for later, helps to break the contradictor of this world-pest.

[kap2:171]
What impression such a stroke exercises on the senses of the broad mass, of the followers as well as of the opponents, can then only measure he who knows the soul of a people not out of books, but out of life. For while in the rows of their followers the attained victory now counts as a triumph of the right of their own cause, the beaten opponent despairs in most cases on the succeeding of a further resistance in general.

[kap2:172]
The more I learned to know above all the methods of the bodily terror, the greater became my plea-for-forgiveness toward the hundreds of thousands who succumbed to it.

[kap2:173]
That I thank most inwardly to my life-time of that time, that it alone has given me back my people; that I learned to distinguish the victims from the seducers.

The Sins of the Bourgeoisdom

[kap2:175]
Otherwise than victims the results of this men-seduction are not to be designated. For if I now in some pictures strove to draw the essence of these "lowest" layers out of life, then this would not be complete without the assurance that I, however, in these depths also found lights again, in the forms of an often rare readiness for sacrifice, faithfulest comradeship, extraordinary sufficiency, and reserved modesty, especially in so far as it concerned the older workerdom of that time. Even if these virtues in the young generation were lost more and more, already through the general influences of the metropolis, there were even here still many with whom the present core-healthy blood became master over the common basenesses of life. If then these often soul-good, upright men in their political activity nevertheless entered into the rows of the mortal enemies of our folkdom and helped to close these, then this lay therein that they indeed neither understood nor could understand the baseness of the new teaching, that no one else took the trouble to care about them, and that finally the social conditions were stronger than any otherwise perhaps present contrary will. The need into which they one day fell, so or so, drove them into the camp of the Social Democracy after all.

[kap2:176]
Since now the bourgeoisdom innumerable times, in the clumsiest but also most immoral manner, made front against even generally humanly justified demands, indeed often without attaining a benefit out of such a posture or even being allowed to expect one in general, even the most decent worker was driven out of the trade-union organisation into political activity.

[kap2:177]
Millions of workers were surely in their inner at first enemies of the social-democratic party, but were defeated in their resistance through a sometimes indeed mad manner and way in which, on the side of the bourgeois parties, position was taken against every demand of a social kind.

The Trade-Union Question

[kap2:179]
The simply bigoted rejection of all attempts at a betterment of the working conditions, of the protective devices on machines, of the suppression of child labour, as well as of the protection of the woman at least in the months when she already carries under her heart the coming people's-comrade, helped along to drive the masses into the net of the Social Democracy, which gratefully grasped every such case of miserable disposition. Never can our political "bourgeoisdom" make good again what was so sinned. For in that it rendered resistance against all attempts at a removal of social abuses, it sowed hatred and seemingly justified itself the assertions of the mortal enemies of the whole folkdom, that only the social-democratic party alone represented the interests of the creating people.

[kap2:180]
It created so in the first line the moral grounding for the actual existence of the trade unions, the organisation that has rendered the political party the greatest driver-services from ever.

[kap2:181]
In my Viennese years of learning I was forced, whether I wanted or not, also to take position on the question of the trade unions.

[kap2:182]
Since I regarded them as an inseparable component of the social-democratic party in itself, my decision was quick and — false.

[kap2:183]
I rejected them self-evidently flatly.

[kap2:184]
Also in this so infinitely important question fate itself gave me instruction.

[kap2:185]
The result was an overturning of my first judgment.

[kap2:186]
With twenty years I had learned to distinguish between the trade union as a means for the defence of general social rights of the employee and for the fighting-out of better life-conditions of the same in the individual, and the trade union as an instrument of the party of the political class fight.

[kap2:187]
That the Social Democracy grasped the enormous meaning of the trade-union movement secured to it the instrument and therewith the success; that the bourgeoisdom did not understand this cost it its political standing. It believed, with a nosy "rejection" of a logical development, to be able to make the end, in order in reality now to force the same into illogical paths. [kap2:188]

It helps so along to create the social presuppositions without which a general national education is not at all to be thought of. It acquires for itself the highest merit, in that, through removal of social cancer-damages, it goes for both spiritual and also bodily sickness-arousers, and so contributes along to a general health of the people's-body.

[kap2:189]
The question after its necessity, then, is really superfluous.

[kap2:190]
So long as there are among employers men with small social understanding, or even lacking right- and fairness-feeling, it is not only the right but the duty of those employed by them, who yet form a part of our folkdom, to protect the interests of the generality against the greed or the unreason of an individual; for the preservation of faith and fidelity on a people's-body is in the interest of the nation just as much as the preservation of the health of the people.

[kap2:191]
Both are heavily threatened through unworthy entrepreneurs who do not feel themselves as members of the whole people's-community. Out of the evil working of their greed or ruthlessness there grow deep damages for the future.

[kap2:192]
To remove the causes of such a development means to acquire for oneself a merit about the nation, and not say reversed.

[kap2:193]
One should thereby not say that it would stand free to every individual to draw the conclusions out of a wrong actually or supposedly done to him, thus to go. No! This is mirror-fencing, and must be regarded as an attempt to divert the attention. Either the removal of bad, unsocial processes is located in the interest of the nation, or not. If yes, then the fight against them must be taken up with the weapons that offer the prospect of success. The individual worker, however, is never in the position to assert himself against the power of the great entrepreneur, since it here cannot concern a question of the victory of the higher right — since indeed at recognition of the same the whole strife, as a consequence of the lack of every occasion, would not be present at all — but the question of the greater power. In the other case, the present feeling of right alone would already end the strife in an honest manner, or, more rightly, it could never come to such a one.

[kap2:194]
No; when unsocial or unworthy treatment of men calls out to resistance, then this fight, so long as no legal, judicial offices for the removal of these damages are created, can only through the greater power come to decision. Therewith, however, it is self-evident that to the individual person, and consequently the concentrated force of the enterprise alone, the number of the employees, gathered together to the individual person, can step opposite, in order not from the beginning already to have to renounce the possibility of victory.

[kap2:195]
So the trade-union organisation can lead to a strengthening of the social thought in its practical effect in daily life, and therewith to a removal of irritation-causes that ever again give the occasion to discontent and to complaints.

[kap2:196]
That it is not so comes to a very great part onto the guilt-account of those who understood how to lay hindrances in the way of every legal regulation of social abuses, or suppressed them by means of their political influence.

The Politicisation of the Trade Unions

[kap2:198]
In just the measure in which the political bourgeoisdom then did not understand the meaning of the trade-union organisation, or, better, did not want to understand it and braced itself to resistance against it, the Social Democracy took charge of the contested movement. It created therewith far-seeingly a firm foundation, which already some times in critical hours proved itself as the last support. Truly, therewith the inner purpose gradually went under, in order to give room to new goals.

[kap2:199]
The Social Democracy never thought of preserving to the professional movement embraced by it its original task.

[kap2:200]
No, so it indeed did not mean this.

[kap2:201]
In a few decades there had become, under its knowing hand, out of the auxiliary means of a defence of social human rights the instrument for the shattering of the national economy. The interests of the workers were thereby not to hinder it in the slightest. For also politically the application of economic pressure-means permits at any time to practise extortions, in so far as only the necessary consciencelessness on the one side and stupid sheep-patience on the other side are present in sufficient measure. Something that in this case applies on both sides.

Already about the turn of the century the trade-union movement had long ceased to serve its earlier task. From year to year it had come more and more into the spell-circle of social-democratic politics, in order finally to find application only still as the ram of the class fight. It was to bring the whole laboriously built-up economic body, through lasting thrusts, finally to collapse, in order so to be able to add more easily the same fate to the state building, after withdrawal of its economic foundation-walls. The representation of all real needs of the workerdom came thereby ever less into question, until political cleverness finally let it appear no longer at all desirable to remove the social and even cultural needs of the broad mass, since one otherwise ran the danger of no longer being able to use these, satisfied in their wishes, as will-less fight-troop eternally further.

[kap2:204]
A development of this kind, forebodingly scented, chased such fear into the class-fighting leaders that they finally, short-handedly, rejected every really blessingful social lifting, indeed took position against it in the most resolved manner.

[kap2:205]
For a grounding of a supposedly so incomprehensible behaviour they thereby never needed to be anxious.

[kap2:206]
In that one spanned the demands ever higher, the possible fulfilment of the same appeared so small and insignificant that one could at any time talk the mass into believing that it here concerned only the devilish attempt, through such a ridiculous satisfaction of holiest rights, to weaken the thrust-force of the workerdom in a cheap manner, indeed, if possible, to paralyse it. At the small thinking-capability of the broad mass, one should not wonder over the success.

[kap2:342]
Should to this people, which eternally lives only upon this earth, the earth be spoken as reward?

[kap2:343]
Have we objective right to the fight for our self-preservation, or is also this only subjectively grounded in us?

[kap2:344]
In that I deepened myself into the teaching of Marxism and so subjected the working of the Jewish people in quiet clarity to a consideration, fate itself gave me its answer.

[kap2:345]
The Jewish teaching of Marxism rejects the aristocratic principle of nature and sets in place of the eternal privilege of force and strength the mass of number and its dead weight. It so denies in man the value of the person, contests the meaning of folkdom and race, and withdraws from mankind therewith the presupposition of its existence and its culture. It would, as the foundation of the universe, lead to the end of every order graspable in thought for men. And just as in this greatest recognisable organism only chaos could be the result of the application of such a law, so on earth for the inhabitants of this star only their own downfall.

[kap2:346]
If the Jew, with the help of his Marxist confession of faith, conquers over the peoples of this world, then his crown will be the death-dance of mankind; then this planet will again, as once before millions of years, draw man-empty through the ether.

[kap2:347]
Eternal nature avenges unrelentingly the transgression of her commandments.

[kap2:348]
So I believe today to act in the sense of the almighty Creator: In that I defend myself against the Jew, I fight for the work of the Lord.
