"Republic"

So I found myself at least on the way of betterment, when the monstrous happened.

[kap7:118]
My first hope was still always that with the land-treason it could only be a matter of a more or less local thing. I tried also to strengthen some comrades in this direction. Especially my Bavarian hospital comrades were to this more than accessible. The mood there was everything other rather than "revolutionary." I could not conceive to myself that also in Munich the madness would break out. The loyalty to the venerable house of Wittelsbach seemed to me then indeed firmer than the will of some Jews. So I could not do otherwise than believe that it was a matter of a putsch of the navy, which in the next days would be struck down.

[kap7:119]
The next days came, and with them the most frightful certainty of my life. Ever more oppressive now became the rumours. What I had held for a local thing should be a general revolution. Thereto came the shameful news from the front. One wanted to capitulate. Yes, was such a thing at large even only possible?

[kap7:120]
On 10 November the pastor came into the hospital for a little address; now we learned everything.

[kap7:121]
I was, to the utmost excited, also present at the short speech. The old, worthy gentleman seemed much to tremble when he communicated to us that the house of Hohenzollern now might no longer bear the German imperial crown, that the fatherland had become "republic," that one had to ask the Almighty not to refuse his blessing to this change and not to want to leave our people in the coming times. He could thereby indeed not do otherwise; he had in few words to think of the royal house, wanted to honour its merits in Pomerania, in Prussia, no, around the German fatherland, and — there he began softly to weep into himself — in the little hall, however, there laid itself deepest downcastness indeed upon all hearts, and I believe that no eye was able to hold back the tears. When, however, the old gentleman tried to tell further and began to communicate that we now had to end the long war, indeed that our fatherland for the future, since the war now would be lost and we gave ourselves into the mercy of the victors, would be exposed to heavy oppressions, that the armistice should be accepted in trust on the magnanimity of our hitherto enemies — there I held it no longer out. To me it became impossible to remain still longer. While around my eyes it became black again, I groped and reeled back to the sleeping hall, threw myself on my camp and dug the burning head into blanket and pillow.

[kap7:122]
Since the day on which I had stood at the grave of the mother I had no more wept. When in my youth fate grasped me unmercifully hard, my defiance grew. When in the long war years death fetched so many a dear comrade and friend out of our rows, it would have appeared to me almost as a sin to lament — died they indeed for Germany! And when finally myself — still in the last days of the frightful wrestling — the creeping gas attacked and began to eat itself into the eyes and I under the terror of blinding for ever for a moment wanted to despair, there the voice of conscience thundered at me: Miserable

[kap7:123]
whiner, do you perhaps want to howl while it goes a hundredfold worse with thousands than with you. And so I then bore, dull and dumb, my lot. Now, however, I could no longer otherwise. Now I first saw how very all personal sorrow sinks over against the misfortune of the fatherland.

[kap7:124]
In Vain All Sacrifices

[kap7:125]
It was therefore all in vain been. In vain all the sacrifices and privations, in vain the hunger and thirst of sometimes endless months, in vain the hours in which we, clawed around by death-anxiety, nevertheless did our duty, and in vain the death of two millions who died thereby. Had not the graves of all the hundreds of thousands to open themselves, who in the belief in the fatherland once had drawn out, in order never to return again? Had they not to open themselves and send the dumb, mud- and blood-covered heroes as revenge-spirits into the homeland, which had so mockingly deceived them around the highest sacrifice which on this world the man is able to bring to his people? Had they died for that, the soldiers of August and September 1914; drew for that the volunteer regiments in the autumn of the same year after the old comrades? Sank for that these boys of seventeen years into the Flemish earth? Was this the sense of the sacrifice which the German mother brought to the fatherland when she with sore heart then let the dearest boys draw, in order never to see them again? Did all this happen for that, that now a heap of miserable criminals was able to lay hand on the fatherland? Had the German soldier therefore in sunbrand and snowstorm, hungering, thirsting, and freezing, tired from sleepless nights and endless marches, held out? Had he therefore lain in the hell of drum fire and in the fever of the gas fight, without yielding, always mindful of the single duty to preserve the fatherland before the entry of the enemy? Truly, also these heroes deserved a stone:

[kap7:126]
"Stranger, who comest to Germany, report to the homeland that we lie here, loyal to the fatherland and obedient to duty."

[kap7:127]
And the homeland?

[kap7:128]
Alone — was it only the single sacrifice which we had to weigh? Was the past Germany less worth? Was there not also an obligation over against one's own history? Were we still worth to relate the fame of the past also to us? How, however, was this deed to be submitted to the future for justification?

[kap7:129]
Miserable and decayed criminals!

[kap7:130]
Resolution to Become a Politician

[kap7:131]
The more I in this hour tried to become clear to myself over the monstrous event, the more there burned to me the shame of indignation and of disgrace in the brow. What was the whole pain of the eyes against this misery?

[kap7:132]
What followed were frightful days and still eviler nights — I knew that everything was lost. To hope on the mercy of the enemy at most fools could manage, or — liars and criminals. In these nights there grew in me the hate, the hate against the originators of this deed.

[kap7:133]
In the days thereupon also my fate became conscious to me. I now had to laugh at the thought of my own future, which a short time ago had still prepared me such bitter cares. Was it not for laughing, to want to build houses on such a ground? Finally it also became clear to me that yet only that had entered which I so often already had feared, only feeling-wise never had been able to believe.

[kap7:134]
Kaiser Wilhelm II had as first German Kaiser stretched the hand of reconciliation to the leaders of Marxism, without suspecting that rogues possess no honour. While they still held the imperial hand in theirs, the other already sought after the dagger.

[kap7:135]
With the Jew there is no pact-making, but only the hard either-or. I, however, resolved to become a politician.
